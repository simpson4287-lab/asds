Another boy convinced me to take her phone as part of a so-called plan to get her attention. I trusted him, but he later stole the phone himself and threatened me into silence. I was manipulated and blamed, left feeling frightened and ashamed. In hindsight, this reflected both my naïve trust in others and my difficulty recognising manipulative intent — traits commonly seen in autistic individuals.

Being consistently overlooked and excluded — especially in school sports — deeply affected my self-esteem. Each experience of being left out reinforced a belief that I wasn’t good enough or capable of belonging. What I couldn’t express then was that I simply processed information and social situations differently. Without recognition or support, I internalised these experiences as personal failures. Between the ages of three and four, I developed a strong attachment to a comfort blanket that became essential to my sense of safety and calm. In hindsight, this fits with broader autistic traits involving differences in sensory and neurological arousal. My sensory experiences as a child were heightened and often overwhelming. Sudden noises like barking dogs, sirens, or motorbikes would make me cover my ears or cry. I quickly began using complex words and phrases for my age, showing an unusual verbal intelligence despite earlier silence. 2.4 	Cognitive and Behavioural Patterns
2.4.1	Early Rigidity and Rule Dependence
From a young age, I showed a strong preference for routines and clear rules. Predictability made me feel safe. Looking back, it was a clear example of my dependence on routine and sensory consistency to maintain emotional regulation.
2.4.2 	Attention to Detail and Hyperfocus
Even as a child, I paid intense attention to small details — noticing things that others missed. Whether it was the texture of fabrics, tiny patterns on walls, or sounds in the background, my focus would narrow until the outside world faded away. At around nine years old, I discovered fishing at an open day with my father and was instantly captivated. It became both a passion and a form of solitary comfort, combining my love of calm environments with deep, focused learning.

2.4.3 	Early Signs of Masking and Camouflage
By primary school, I had begun masking instinctively — copying the speech and behaviour of others to blend in. I observed how classmates interacted and tried to mirror them, but it never felt natural. While this helped me avoid attention, it also created a sense of separation from my authentic self. Even then, I experienced exhaustion from trying to act 'normal,' though I didn’t yet understand why.
2.5 	Routine, Structure, and Predictability
Routine was my anchor throughout childhood. I felt safest when days followed a predictable pattern and events unfolded as expected. Changes to plans, school schedules, or family routines caused significant distress. I would try to regain control by recreating order — rewriting lists, tidying spaces, or repeating actions that made me feel calm. Predictability provided emotional safety in an otherwise uncertain world.
2.6. 	Summary
Looking back from adulthood, my childhood experiences reveal a consistent pattern of autistic traits across sensory, emotional, and social domains. I displayed heightened sensitivity to sensory input, a strong attachment to comforting textures, delayed interoceptive awareness, and difficulty interpreting social dynamics. I also relied heavily on familiar routines and rituals — such as the 'Crystal Maze and custard' routine — to create a sense of control and comfort. Emotional expression was limited by confusion and overload, and I preferred solitary, predictable environments such as fishing and online chess, where I could engage deeply without social pressure.

The exclusion I experienced in social and physical activities, particularly in sports and peer relationships, had a lasting effect on self-esteem. I was vulnerable to manipulation and misunderstanding due to my trusting nature and lack of awareness of others’ motives. These experiences were not the result of disinterest or defiance but of a brain that processed the world differently — one that sought predictability, fairness, and sensory balance. Although misunderstood at the time, these traits now make sense as part of an autistic profile that shaped both my challenges and the ways I learned to cope.

In hindsight, understanding these early experiences brings compassion to my younger self and clarity to the lifelong patterns that followed. These observations, viewed retrospectively, are consistent with a presentation of Autism Spectrum Disorder in early childhood and align with the characteristics recognised in later life.

I’ve spent most of my life masking—adopting behaviors to appear socially “normal” or to meet others’ expectations. Masking drains me completely, and when I’m alone afterward, I often collapse into exhaustion or emotional flatness. 1.7.2 	Preference for Order and Predictability

1.8 	Interests and Focused Activities

Looking back from adulthood, my childhood experiences reveal a consistent pattern of autistic traits across sensory, emotional, and social domains.
