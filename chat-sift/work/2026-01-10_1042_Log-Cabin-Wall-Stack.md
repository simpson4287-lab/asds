For an external **log-cabin wall with an insulated stud wall** — whether you’re upgrading an existing garden cabin for *year-round* use or building a *drop-in internal insulated frame* — the “right stack” (layer sequence) needs to address **thermal performance, moisture control, movement of the log structure, and finishing**. In the UK context this also means planning for thermal bridging and vapour control appropriate to timber structures.

Below is a **practical insulated stud wall build-up** that works well on the *inside* of a log wall (typical retrofit/upgrade approach) and keeps thermal performance high while avoiding moisture problems.

---

## Typical Insulated Stud Wall Stack (Internal, against log wall)

**From inside (room side) to the log wall:**

1. **Internal finish**
   * Plasterboard (12.5 mm standard) or tongue-and-groove pine/OSB
   * Optional vapour control layer taped at joints (especially if heating regularly)

2. **Stud frame**
   * 45 × 70 mm or 45 × 95 mm vertical timber studs
   * Spaced at 400–600 mm centres (to match insulation batts) citeturn0search1

3. **Insulation within stud cavities**
   * Mineral wool (e.g., Rockwool) batts sized to fit stud depth  
     * Or **PIR rigid boards** for higher performance in thinner cavities citeturn0search7turn0search12
   * Aim for at least **75–100 mm effective insulated depth** for heated spaces in the UK

4. **Breathable membrane / air barrier**
   * A thin, vapour-permeable membrane between insulation and log wall
   * Allows the log wall to “breathe” while controlling moisture back into the stud wall

5. **Gap/ventilation cavity (optional but recommended)**
   * Small service gap (10–20 mm) between breathable membrane and log surface
   * Reduces moisture build-up and allows any condensation to dry

6. **Log cabin structural wall**
   * Your existing log wall (e.g., 44 mm or thicker logs typical for year-round use) citeturn0search1

---

## External Finish Options (if building outward-facing stud wall)

If you are adding an **external stud wall around a log shell**, the same principles apply, but layers go from outside inward:

1. **External weather cladding**
   * Timber weatherboards, shiplap, or engineered cladding

2. **Breather membrane**
   * Weather-resistant yet vapour-permeable

3. **External stud frame with insulation**
   * Studs + insulation (mineral wool or PIR)

4. **Internal membrane / air barrier**
   * If required by local regs/condensation risk assessment

5. **Internal finish**
   * Plasterboard or timber lining

This approach effectively creates a **ventilated and insulated timber-frame envelope over the log shell**, respecting the breathability of the original logwork.

---

## Key Performance & Building Considerations

### Insulation Type
- **Mineral wool (e.g., Rockwool)**  
  • Good thermal, acoustic and fire performance in timber frame walls. citeturn0search7

- **PIR rigid foam board**  
  • Higher R-value per mm, useful if space is limited. citeturn0search12

### Moisture & Breathability
- Log walls move and breathe; avoid complete airtight seals directly onto the log  
  • Provide a permeable layer and ventilation gap to mitigate condensation risk. citeturn0search1

### Allow for Log Settling
- When stud framing inside a log cabin, do not fix rigidly to the log surface
  • Use slotted timber fixing methods so the log can contract/expand without stressing the new stud wall. citeturn0search4

### Thickness & U-Values
- Thicker insulation yields better U-values, but balance against usable internal space
- Typical retrofit thickness: **70–100 mm+** inside studs for cold UK climates

**External insulated stud wall**  
Cladding → Breather membrane → Studs with insulation → Air control membrane (if applicable) → Log wall

For **external studwork** (or an internal stud wall treated as the “external face” of the insulated build-up), the Tyvek is **mechanically fixed, then sealed**, not glued. The sequence matters.

Below is the **correct, standard method** used in timber-frame construction.

---

## 1. Orientation
- Tyvek is **non-directional** (no “wrong” face)
- Roll it **horizontally**, starting at the bottom of the wall and working up
- This ensures laps shed moisture correctly

---

## 2. Primary Fixing to Studs
Tyvek is fixed **directly to the stud frame**, before battens or cladding.

**Fixings:**
- Galvanised staples **or**
- Stainless / galvanised clout nails with plastic caps (preferred externally)

**Spacing:**
- Around **150–300 mm centres** along studs
- More frequent at edges and openings

The fixings are **temporary in function** — final clamping is done by battens.

---

## 3. Laps and Sealing (critical)
- Horizontal laps: **minimum 100 mm**
- Vertical laps: **minimum 150 mm**
- Tape all overlaps with:
  - Tyvek tape **or**
  - A compatible acrylic breather-membrane tape

This is what provides:
- Wind-tightness
- Air-tightness
- Controlled vapour escape

---

## 4. Detailing Around Openings
- Wrap Tyvek **into** window and door reveals
- Tape corners carefully (no straight cuts into corners)
- Bottom of openings should shed water outward (no reverse laps)

---

## 5. Counter-Battens / Service Battens (very important)
After Tyvek is fixed:

- Fix **vertical battens** (typically 25 × 38 mm) through the membrane into studs
- These battens:
  - Clamp the Tyvek permanently
  - Create a **ventilated cavity**
  - Provide a fixing zone for cladding or secondary structure

Once battens are installed, the staples are no longer structurally relevant.

---

## 6. Final External Layer
- Timber cladding, composite boards, or outer sheathing
- Fixed to the battens, **not through the membrane**

---

## If this is INTERNAL to a log cabin
Same principle, with two caveats:

1. Battens can be thinner (10–25 mm) — you only need airflow, not drainage
2. Studs must allow **log movement**:
   - Slotted fixings, or
   - Floating head detail

---

## What ventilated cavity battens are

They are **thin timber battens** fixed **over the breather membrane (Tyvek)** and **into the studs**, creating a continuous air gap between the membrane and the external cladding.

Their functions are:
- Provide a **drainage plane** for any water that gets past the cladding  
- Allow **air movement** so moisture can dry  
- Clamp the membrane securely (staples are not structural)  
- Provide a fixing zone for external boards

---

### **Minimum (internal / sheltered / not visible)**
- **10–25 mm depth**
- 25–38 mm width
- Adequate if fully sheltered and not directly weather-exposed

### **Recommended / best practice**
- **25 × 38 mm** treated softwood battens  
This is the standard timber-frame cavity batten size in the UK.

Depth is what matters — **25 mm gives reliable airflow**.

---

## Orientation (this matters)

### Behind sheet materials (OSB, fibre cement)
- **Vertical battens**
- Run from bottom to top
- This allows gravity drainage and stack ventilation

### Behind horizontal boards
- Still use **vertical battens**
- If horizontal battens are required for fixing, add a **secondary horizontal layer** over the verticals

Never block vertical airflow with horizontal-only battens.

---

- Fix **through Tyvek → into studs**
- Stainless or galvanised screws
- Typical spacing: **400–600 mm centres**
- Do not overtighten — clamp, don’t crush

The battens permanently secure the membrane.

### Bottom of wall
- Leave a **10–20 mm opening**
- Fit insect mesh if required
- Do not seal shut

### Top of wall
- Leave a corresponding **exit gap**
- Allows convective airflow (chimney effect)

This turns the cavity into a **working ventilation path**, not a dead void.

## Why OSB gets a bad reputation

OSB fails when **one or more of these are true**:
- It is **directly exposed** to rain
- It is **behind a failed or missing membrane**
- It has **no ventilated cavity**
- Cut edges are left **unsealed**
- Water is trapped with **no drying path**

In those cases, OSB will swell, lose strength, and eventually decay.

That is a **detailing failure**, not a material failure.

Your proposed stack is:

External cladding  
→ ventilated cavity battens  
→ Tyvek breather membrane  
→ **OSB (optional / sacrificial)**  
→ insulated stud wall  

In this configuration:

- OSB is **never in contact with liquid water**
- Tyvek stops wind-driven rain
- The cavity allows **any incidental moisture to drain and dry**
- Vapour can move **outwards**, not get trapped

Under these conditions, OSB3 is operating **exactly as designed**.

This is standard UK timber-frame practice.
