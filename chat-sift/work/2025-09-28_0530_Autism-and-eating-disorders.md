Can people with level 1 autism develop eating disorders, why?

[M2] AI (2025-09-28 05:30):
Yes, people with Level 1 autism (sometimes referred to as “high-functioning autism”) can develop eating disorders, and there are several reasons why they might be at higher risk compared to neurotypical individuals. The risk isn’t universal, but certain traits associated with autism can contribute. Here’s a detailed breakdown:  

---

### **1. Sensory sensitivities**
- Many people with autism experience heightened sensitivity to textures, smells, tastes, and even the appearance of food.  
- This can lead to **extreme selective eating** or avoidance of certain food groups, which may resemble restrictive eating patterns seen in anorexia or avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID).  

### **2. Need for routine and rigidity**
- Level 1 autism often involves a strong preference for routines and predictability.  
- This can manifest in strict eating routines, limiting foods to only “safe” options, or rituals around meals.  
- Over time, rigid avoidance of foods can escalate into a full-blown restrictive eating disorder.  

### **3. Anxiety and control**
- Anxiety is common in autism. Some individuals use control over food as a way to manage overwhelming stress or uncertainty in other areas of life.  
- Restricting food intake or obsessing over certain “safe” foods can become a coping mechanism.  

### **4. Social and body image factors**
- While body image issues are less common than in classic anorexia, some autistic individuals—especially adolescents and young adults—may internalize societal pressures or develop obsessive concerns about weight or shape.  
- Difficulties with social understanding can exacerbate feelings of isolation, which can contribute to disordered eating patterns.  

### **5. Co-occurring conditions**
- ADHD, anxiety disorders, OCD, depression, and sensory processing disorder are common comorbidities in autism and can increase the likelihood of eating disorders.  
- For example, OCD tendencies can make someone fixate on “safe” foods or calorie counts.  

### **6. Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID)**
- ARFID is an eating disorder that’s particularly common in autistic individuals.  
- It’s characterized by avoidance of food due to sensory issues, fear of adverse consequences (like choking), or lack of interest in eating—without the body image concerns seen in anorexia.  

---

**Summary:**  
People with Level 1 autism can develop eating disorders due to a combination of sensory sensitivities, need for control and routine, anxiety, co-occurring mental health conditions, and social challenges. The disorders may present differently than in neurotypical people—for example, being more focused on sensory or routine factors than weight or shape concerns.
