In UK drug driving law (**s.5A Road Traffic Act 1988**), the court must impose **at least 12 months’ disqualification** *unless* there is a legally recognised **“special reason”** not to.  

---

## 🔹 What is a “Special Reason”?
- It must be **a mitigating or extenuating circumstance**, directly connected to the offence itself.  
- It must be **exceptional and unusual**, not just ordinary hardship.  
- It must **not amount to a defence** (i.e. you still committed the offence).  
- It must be recognised in law by case precedent.  

---

## 🔹 Common Examples of “Special Reasons” in Drug/Drink Driving
1. **Spiked Drink / Unknowingly Drugged**  
   - If someone else laced your food/drink with drugs without your knowledge.  
   - You must prove you had no reason to suspect and couldn’t have known.  

2. **Medical Emergency**  
   - Driving because of a genuine life-or-death emergency (e.g. rushing someone to hospital).  
   - Must show there was no reasonable alternative.  

3. **Shortness of Distance Driven**  
   - If the driving was only for a very short distance, with minimal risk to others.  
   - E.g. moving a car a few metres off a dangerous spot.  

4. **No Likelihood of Driving Again** (rare)  
   - Usually applies where the person has an exceptional reason, e.g. terminal illness.  

---

## 🔹 What **Doesn’t** Count
- Risk of losing your job  
- Needing your car for family responsibilities  
- Claiming you “felt fine” to drive  
- First offence / good character (these are mitigation, not special reasons)  

---

## 🔹 Effect if Accepted
- Court can **reduce or even avoid the disqualification entirely**.  
- Still convicted of the offence, but the punishment is far lighter.  
- Hard to prove — usually requires strong evidence (e.g. witness statements, toxicology, medical proof).  

---

⚖️ In practice, special reasons are **very rare**. Most defendants argue mitigation instead (to reduce to 12 months, then take the rehab course for 9 months).

Good question — “discharge” in criminal cases can be confusing because it doesn’t mean the person is found not guilty. It’s still a conviction, but it’s the **lightest sentence available**.  

---

## ⚖️ **Two Types of Discharge in UK Criminal Law**

### 1. **Absolute Discharge**
- Court decides that although you’re guilty, **no punishment is appropriate**.  
- Goes on your criminal record, but there’s no fine, no community order, no probation.  
- Used where the offence is very minor or where punishment would be unjust.  
- Example: someone technically guilty but with such exceptional circumstances (e.g. severe mental health crisis) that further punishment isn’t in the public interest.  

---

### 2. **Conditional Discharge**
- You are found guilty but discharged on condition you **don’t commit another offence within a set period** (up to 3 years).  
- If you reoffend in that period, the court can re-sentence you for the original offence as well as the new one.  
- If you don’t reoffend, the discharge effectively lapses with no further penalty.  

---

## 🔹 Important Points
- A discharge is still a **criminal conviction** (but usually seen as very minor).  
- It still appears on a criminal record, though it becomes **spent immediately** for absolute discharge, and after the conditional period for conditional discharge.  
- In **drug driving cases**, discharges are rare because Parliament set a **mandatory 12-month disqualification** — the court can’t get around that with a discharge.  
  - They could give an **absolute/conditional discharge** *instead of a fine or community penalty*, but the **driving ban still applies** unless a “special reason” is proven.  

---

✅ In plain terms:  
- **Absolute discharge** = guilty but no punishment.  
- **Conditional discharge** = guilty, no immediate punishment unless you offend again.
