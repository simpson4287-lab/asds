### 1️⃣ Purpose and Overview
Module 4 is used when the person being assessed can **speak in full, complex sentences** and can discuss experiences, ideas, and emotions.  
It differs from the child modules, which rely on play or toys. Instead, Module 4 uses **conversation-based activities** that test how naturally and flexibly you communicate, connect, and respond in social dialogue.  

It’s designed to reveal how you use **language, tone, gestures, eye contact, and empathy** in realistic discussion — not to test intelligence or vocabulary. The examiner looks at *how* you share thoughts, *how much* awareness you show of the listener, and whether social reciprocity (give-and-take) happens smoothly or feels mechanical.  

Each section creates a mild “social pressure” — for instance, being asked to describe a feeling or narrate an event — to see what your **spontaneous social communication** looks like when gently challenged.

---

### 2️⃣ Setting and Preparation
The session feels like a structured conversation in an interview or counselling room.  
- You and the examiner sit facing each other, close enough for eye contact.  
- There’s usually a small table with some objects, pictures, or a short illustrated storybook, but no toys.  
- The space is quiet and minimal so distractions are limited.  

The examiner controls when each task begins, sometimes bringing out materials one at a time. They will usually **start with relaxed small talk** to lower anxiety and watch your natural speech rhythm.

---

### 3️⃣ How the Session Flows
A typical Module 4 session lasts **40 – 60 minutes** and feels like a guided conversation with short creative or reflective tasks.  

It usually follows this pattern:

| Stage | Aim |
|-------|-----|
| **1. Warm-up** | Establish comfort, observe spontaneous talk and social cues. |
| **2. Structured tasks** | 10-15 short activities exploring storytelling, description, social thinking, and emotions. |
| **3. Closing discussion** | Light talk to help you decompress and end naturally. |

The examiner doesn’t just record what you say, but also **how**:  
- Do you pick up on tone and rhythm of normal conversation?  
- Do you elaborate when prompted or give brief literal replies?  
- Do you recognise subtle social cues, such as humour or a change in mood?

### 4️⃣ What Each Activity Involves

#### **1. Construction Task (optional)**
You might be asked to assemble or organise a small set of objects (like blocks, shapes, or a simple model).  
- **Purpose:** to watch problem-solving, planning, and cooperation.  
- **Observation focus:** whether you ask clarifying questions, share what you’re doing, or work silently. People with autism sometimes focus on accuracy but overlook social engagement (eye contact, small talk, asking if they’re “doing it right”).  

---

#### **2. Tell a Story from a Book**
You’re shown a short illustrated children’s story or picture sequence and asked to tell the story in your own words.  
- **Purpose:** to test narrative organisation, imagination, and emotional insight.  
- **What’s noticed:** how you infer what characters might think or feel, how logically you link events, and whether you add empathy (“He was embarrassed when…”) or stay factual (“He dropped it”).  
- **Clinical idea:** Adults on the spectrum often describe *what happened* clearly but skip *why* it mattered emotionally.

---

#### **3. Describe a Picture (optional)**
You describe a picture or scene (for example, a group of people interacting).  
- **Purpose:** to evaluate perception of social context — can you notice relationships, emotions, or implied motives?  
- **Observation:** Do you see the “big picture” or focus on minor details like colours or numbers?  
This reveals whether social meaning or surface features dominate your attention.

---

#### **4. Conversation and Narration of Events**
This is the core part — an open conversation about your **everyday life**, such as work, studies, routines, or recent experiences.  
- **Purpose:** to see how you maintain dialogue and adjust to the examiner’s cues.  
- **Examiner’s goal:** to watch whether conversation feels reciprocal (two-way), or like alternating monologues.  
- **Scored areas:**  
  - Turn-taking and topic flow.  
  - Appropriateness of detail and humour.  
  - Responsiveness to feedback (do you notice if the listener seems bored or confused?).  
  - Variety in tone, facial expression, and body language.  

---

#### **5. Work or School Discussion (optional)**
You might be asked about your current or past employment or studies.  
- **Observation:** how you explain responsibilities, relationships, and problem-solving.  
People with ASD often describe rules and tasks precisely but struggle to express how they feel about colleagues or teamwork.

---

#### **6. Social Difficulties and Annoyances**
Here you discuss everyday frustrations — such as misunderstandings or social conflicts.  
- **Purpose:** to examine self-awareness and ability to read social dynamics.  
- **Observed:** Do you recognise how others might perceive you, or focus only on your own point of view?  
- **Diagnostic value:** difficulty identifying emotional reactions in others often shows here.

---

#### **7. Emotions**
You’re asked to talk about times you felt proud, happy, angry, embarrassed, or sad.  
- **Observation:** whether you use appropriate emotional language, tone, and expression.  
- **Clinically:** Individuals on the spectrum may describe events in logical rather than emotional terms (“I scored well on the test” instead of “I was excited and relieved”).  
The examiner notes not just words but body language and prosody (intonation).

---

#### **8. Demonstration Task**
You’re invited to *show* how to do something you know well — e.g., “Explain how to make your favourite drink” or “Show me how to tie a knot.”  
- **Purpose:** to assess communication clarity, sequencing, and checking the listener’s understanding.  
- **Key behaviour:** do you glance up to see if the examiner follows? adjust your explanation?  
Autistic communicators often instruct accurately but may not monitor listener comprehension.

---

#### **9. Comic Strips or Vignettes (optional)**
You’re shown short cartoon panels illustrating a social scenario.  
- **Purpose:** to see how well you interpret non-verbal or implied humour and the perspectives of others.  
- **Observed:** whether you can explain *why* something is funny or awkward, not only *what* is happening.  

---

#### **10. Break**
A short pause where the examiner observes natural relaxation or self-soothing behaviours — tapping, pacing, limited conversation. This helps gauge anxiety regulation and transition flexibility.

---

#### **11. Daily Living (optional)**
You’re asked about daily routines, independence, cooking, cleaning, or finances.  
- **Purpose:** to assess practical organisation and adaptive living.  
- **Observation:** balance between independence and social understanding — e.g., recognising shared household expectations.

---

#### **12. Friendships, Relationships, and Marriage**
One of the most informative tasks.  
- **Purpose:** to explore social reciprocity, empathy, and understanding of intimacy.  
- **Typical prompts:**  
  - “Do you have close friends?”  
  - “What makes a good friend?”  
  - “Have you ever been in a relationship?”  
- **Examiner’s focus:**  
  - Are descriptions idealised or mechanical?  
  - Do you understand mutual emotional needs?  
  - Can you distinguish between acquaintance and genuine closeness?  

---

#### **13. Loneliness**
You’re asked whether you ever feel lonely and what you do about it.  
- **Purpose:** to see if you experience and can articulate social isolation or emotional pain.  
- **Observed:** whether loneliness is recognised as emotional (“I miss people”) or purely factual (“I don’t go out much”).  

---

#### **14. Future Plans and Aspirations**
Discussion about hopes, ambitions, and personal goals.  
- **Purpose:** to evaluate self-concept and ability to project into the future.  
- **Observation:** whether goals are realistic, personally meaningful, and socially grounded.  
Autistic adults sometimes describe technical or solitary ambitions with little emotional context; others may struggle to envision the future.

---

#### **15. Inventing a Story**
You’re asked to make up a brief story from random objects or ideas.  
- **Purpose:** to assess imagination, flexibility, and creativity in social storytelling.  
- **Observation:** richness of narrative, inclusion of emotions, and coherence.  
Some individuals narrate literal or rule-based stories lacking psychological depth; others may create highly detailed but repetitive plots.

### 5️⃣ What the Examiner Is Evaluating

All activities together provide a multi-dimensional picture of:
| Domain | What is Examined | Typical Indicators of Difficulty |
|---------|------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Communication** | Verbal fluency, coherence, use of gestures, tone, ability to adapt explanations. | Speech may be overly formal, monotone, or pedantic; limited gestures; poor monitoring of listener. |
| **Reciprocal Social Interaction** | Quality of emotional connection, shared enjoyment, empathy, eye contact, response to humour or empathy. | Interaction feels one-sided, limited emotional warmth, weak understanding of social nuance. |
| **Imagination / Creativity** | Flexibility, spontaneous storytelling, ability to infer others’ thoughts. | Stories lack inner life; thinking rigid or repetitive. |
| **Restricted Interests & Behaviours** | Over-focus on particular topics, repetitive speech patterns, limited perspective-taking. | Topic fixation, tangential comments, difficulty shifting. |

The examiner codes each observable behaviour immediately after the session using a 0–3 scale:  
- **0 = No abnormality / behaviour typical**  
- **1 = Slightly unusual or reduced**  
- **2 = Clearly atypical**  
- **3 = Markedly abnormal or interferes with reciprocity**

### 6️⃣ Scoring and Diagnostic Algorithm
Module 4 combines scores from **Communication (A)** and **Reciprocal Social Interaction (B)** into a **Total (A + B)**.  

- **Autism:** Total ≥ 10  
- **Autism Spectrum:** Total 7–9  
- **Non-Spectrum:** Total ≤ 6  

These cut-offs are used along with other assessments — developmental history, questionnaires, and clinical judgment — to form the final diagnosis.

---

### 7️⃣ Example Outcome
An adult participant might:  
- Speak fluently but formally;  
- Use advanced vocabulary with limited emotional tone;  
- Maintain minimal eye contact;  
- Miss subtle social humour.  

Such a profile may produce high scores in both communication and social reciprocity domains, placing them within the **Autism Spectrum** range, even if everyday functioning appears competent.

---

### 8️⃣ The Clinical Meaning
The ADOS-2 does **not** diagnose by itself.  
It captures how you naturally interact under structured social circumstances.  
Results are integrated with your **developmental history**, **self-report**, and possibly **informant interviews** (parents, partners, or teachers).  

High scores indicate the presence of *observable autistic traits* such as reduced reciprocal communication, restricted emotional expression, or rigid conversational style.  
Lower scores suggest socially typical engagement.

---

### 9️⃣ How to Approach the Assessment
- **Be yourself:** there are no “right” answers.  
- **Expect conversational prompts:** the examiner isn’t judging manners but watching your natural style.  
- **If you pause or go off-topic,** that’s fine — the examiner records how you self-correct or seek feedback.  
- **If a task feels artificial,** that’s intentional; it helps reveal how you respond to subtle social expectations.
