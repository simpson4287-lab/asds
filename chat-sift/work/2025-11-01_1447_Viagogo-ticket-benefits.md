Yes — that’s a **real and widespread practice** on resale platforms like viagogo, StubHub, and SeatGeek, and it’s one of the key reasons consumer advocates advise caution. It’s called **speculative ticket selling** (or “spec listing”), and it works almost exactly as you described.  

Here’s a clear breakdown:

---

### 🎭 What actually happens  
- **A seller lists tickets they don’t yet possess** — often before the event organiser or primary ticketing site (like Ticketmaster) has even released all seats.  
- They advertise specific seats or sections *as if they have them in hand*.  
- Once someone buys, the seller then **tries to obtain** matching or “equivalent” tickets from another source (e.g. via another resale site, a contact, or even later from the official sale).  
- If they succeed, they transfer those tickets to the buyer and **keep the margin** between what they sold for and what they bought for.  
- If they fail, they’re supposed to refund the buyer — but that may happen **right before the event**, leaving the buyer ticketless or scrambling.

---

### ⚠️ Why it’s a problem
1. **Misleading advertising** — you think you’re buying a guaranteed seat that physically exists, when in reality it might not.  
2. **Late or non-delivery** — tickets may arrive only hours before the event, or not at all.  
3. **Seat substitutions** — the seller may send “comparable” but worse seats to fulfil the sale.  
4. **Refund delays** — even though viagogo has a “Guarantee,” users often report that refunds take weeks and can’t be actioned until after the event.  
5. **Inflated market prices** — speculative listings drive perceived scarcity, pushing overall resale prices up.

---

### 🧾 What viagogo says  
- Viagogo’s terms *allow* this practice under its “guarantee framework.” Sellers can list tickets “expected to be obtained,” as long as they can supply equivalent ones later.  
- The company promises buyers will “receive the tickets they ordered in time for the event or receive comparable replacements.”  
- But enforcement relies entirely on viagogo’s internal process — so if a seller fails, your only recourse is through viagogo support, not the event organiser.
