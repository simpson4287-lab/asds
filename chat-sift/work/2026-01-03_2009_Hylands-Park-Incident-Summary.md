10.2 Legal basis: “road or other place to which the public have or are permitted to have access”
Under sections 4 and 5A of the Road Traffic Act 1988, the prosecution must prove that any alleged driving occurred “on a road or other place to which the public have or are permitted to have access.” The meaning of “public place” turns on the access position at the material time rather than ownership alone.
10.3 Ownership and permissive access basis (Open Spaces Act and by-laws)
Hylands Park is owned and managed by Chelmsford City Council under Open Spaces Act 1906, section 12. Public access is granted on a permissive basis via Council by-laws, including Regulation 26 of the 1966 Hylands Park Regulations, which limit opening hours and authorise the Council to close and withdraw permission after a specified time each evening.
The consolidated materials further state:

•	the Council retains express power to close access to the park
10.4 Open gate scenario: physical entry does not equal lawful permission
If a gate or pedestrian entrance remains unlocked after closure, this does not constitute continued public permission to access the park.
Physical entry may be possible, but such access is without lawful authority and amounts to civil trespass. A failure to secure the boundary is an enforcement/administrative lapse and does not convert the land back into a public place for Road Traffic Act purposes.
10.5 Case law summary (consolidated list)
The consolidated materials cite and rely upon authorities confirming the lawful-access principle at the material time:
•	DPP v Vivier [1991] Crim LR 637 – “public place” depends upon public access, not ownership
•	DPP v MacDonagh [1974] RTR 382 – once a gated car park is closed it ceases to be a public place even if physical entry remains possible
•	Laurie v DPP [1989] Crim LR 108 – the question is determined at the time of the alleged offence
•	Robson v Hallett [1967] 2 QB 939 – permission can be revoked; continued presence thereafter is trespass
•	Additional authority referenced across materials: May v DPP (2005) for the proposition that lawful public access is central
10.6 Legal consequence (public place element)
Once Hylands Park is formally closed under its by-laws, the public no longer have permission to enter or remain. The area is therefore not a “public place” within the meaning of the Road Traffic Act 1988. An open gate does not alter this status; it creates an opportunity for unlawful entry, not lawful access. Any alleged driving within the park after closure is therefore advanced as falling outside the statutory definition required for Road Traffic Act offences.

Appendix D: Interpretation Note — Effect of Open Gate on Public Place Status (Hylands Park)
Prepared for: Chris Simpson
Date: 20 October 2025
1. Legal basis
Under sections 4 and 5A of the Road Traffic Act 1988, the prosecution must prove that any alleged driving occurred “on a road or other place to which the public have or are permitted to have access”. The term “public place” has been interpreted by case law as depending upon factual access at the time of the alleged incident rather than ownership alone.
2. Ownership and permission status
Hylands Park is owned and managed by Chelmsford City Council under the Open Spaces Act 1906, section 12. Public access is granted solely on a permissive basis via Council by-laws (Regulation 26 of the 1966 Hylands Park Regulations). Those by-laws expressly limit opening hours and authorise the Council to close and withdraw permission after a specified time each evening.
3. The “open gate” scenario
If a gate or pedestrian entrance remains unlocked after closure, this does not constitute continued public permission to access the park. Physical entry may still be possible, but such access would occur without lawful authority and amounts to civil trespass. A failure to secure the boundary does not convert the land back into a “public place”; it reflects only an administrative or enforcement lapse. The land therefore remains private in law, regardless of its physical accessibility.
4. Case law summary
The following authorities confirm the distinction between lawful and factual access:
•	DPP v Vivier [1991] Crim LR 637 – A “public place” depends upon factual public access, not ownership.
•	DPP v MacDonagh [1974] RTR 382 – A gated car park, once closed, ceased to be a public place even though physical entry was possible.
•	Laurie v DPP [1989] Crim LR 108 – The question of public access is determined at the specific time of the alleged offence.
•	Robson v Hallett [1967] 2 QB 939 – Permission to enter property may be revoked at any moment; continued presence thereafter is trespass.
5. Legal consequence
Once Hylands Park is formally closed under its governing by-laws, the public no longer have permission to enter or remain. The area is therefore not a “public place” within the meaning of the Road Traffic Act 1988. An open gate does not alter this status; it merely creates an opportunity for an unlawful entry, not lawful access. Any alleged driving within the park after closure thus falls outside the statutory definition required for Road Traffic Act offences.
6. Recommended legal wording for solicitor submission
At the material time, Hylands Park was closed pursuant to Chelmsford City Council by-laws enacted under the Open Spaces Act 1906. The park had therefore ceased to be a place to which the public were permitted access. Any driving within it occurred on private land, outside the scope of the Road Traffic Act 1988. The fact that a gate may have been left open does not change the land’s legal character; it reflects only an enforcement omission, not continuing public permission.
7. Summary table
Scenario	Legal Effect
Park open within hours	Public place – lawful access by permission
Park closed (gates locked)	Private land – access withdrawn, not a public place
Gate left unlocked after closure	Private land – physical access possible but unlawful (trespass)
Public unaware of closure	Still private; may mitigate culpability but does not restore public status

# Part E — Legal Analysis (Consolidated)

## 15. Legal requirement: “road or other public place / place to which the public have or are permitted access”
15.1 **Statutory element**  
RTA 1988 offences (including s.4 and related provisions referenced across the materials) require proof that conduct occurred on a road or other place to which the public have or are permitted access. The consolidated position treats this as a strict element: if not met, the offence is not made out.

15.2 **Status of Hylands Park (as asserted in the consolidated materials)**

- Regulation 26: park open to public 7:30 a.m. until one hour after sunset; after that no person shall remain.  
- Published gate closing times (June/July: 22:00) referenced as practical enforcement.  
- Council retains power to close and withdraw permission.

15.3 **Closure and the “open gate” scenario (preserved position)**  
At the material time (approximately 03:30–04:00):  
- The park is asserted to have been lawfully closed.  
- Public permission is asserted to have been withdrawn.  
- Presence after closure is characterised as civil trespass, not lawful public access.  
- An unlocked/open pedestrian gate does not restore lawful permission; it reflects an enforcement/administrative lapse rather than continuing permission.

15.4 **Case law framing cited in the source set (preserved list)**  
- **DPP v Vivier [1991] Crim LR 637** — “public place” depends upon public access, not ownership.  
- **DPP v MacDonagh [1974] RTR 382** — once a gated car park is closed it ceases to be a public place even if physical entry remains possible.  
- **Laurie v DPP [1989] Crim LR 108** — question determined at the time of alleged offence.  
- **Robson v Hallett [1967] 2 QB 939** — permission can be revoked; continued presence thereafter is trespass.  
- **May v DPP (2005)** — referenced for the centrality of lawful public access.

15.5 **Jurisdiction conclusion advanced**  
Driving wholly within Hylands Park during closure hours, where lawful access is withdrawn, is advanced as falling outside “road or other public place” for RTA purposes. This is presented as a **substantive defence** (not merely mitigation).

## 16. Driving vs being “in charge” (statutory structure preserved)
16.1 **Distinction preserved**  
- **s.4(1) Driving while unfit**: proof of actual driving while impaired; no statutory “no likelihood” defence.  
- **s.4(2) Being in charge while unfit**: control/capability to drive; **statutory defence** under **s.4(3)** if no likelihood of driving while unfit (balance of probabilities).

## Appendix D — Interpretation Note: Effect of Open Gate on Public Place Status (Hylands Park) (embedded)
**Prepared for:** Chris Simpson  
**Date:** 20 October 2025  

1. Legal basis: RTA requires driving on road/other place with public permitted access; “public place” depends on access at the time.  
2. Ownership and permission: council-owned under Open Spaces Act 1906 s.12; access is permissive via by-laws including Regulation 26 (closure/withdrawal of permission).  
3. Open gate: physical accessibility does not equal lawful permission; entry after closure is trespass; unsecured boundary does not convert land into a public place.  
4. Case law: Vivier; MacDonagh; Laurie; Robson (as listed in the source note).  
5. Consequence: once closed, not a public place for RTA; open gate does not alter legal status.  
6. Recommended wording: park closed under by-laws; ceased to be permitted public access; driving within it outside RTA; open gate is enforcement omission, not continuing permission.  
7. Summary table:  
- Park open: public place  
- Park closed: private/restricted land, not public place  
- Gate left unlocked after closure: still private; trespass  
- Public unaware: still private; may mitigate but does not restore public status
