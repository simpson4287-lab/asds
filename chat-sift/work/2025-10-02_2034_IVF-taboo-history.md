Yes — when **IVF (in vitro fertilisation)** was first developed in the late 1970s, it was considered quite taboo in many parts of the world.  

Here are some reasons why:  

- **Religious and moral concerns**: Some religious groups strongly opposed IVF, seeing it as “unnatural” or interfering with the natural process of conception. There were also ethical debates about what to do with unused embryos.  
- **Public skepticism and fear**: When Louise Brown, the first “test-tube baby,” was born in 1978, the media used terms that made IVF sound experimental and strange. Some people worried about potential health consequences for the children or framed it as “playing God.”  
- **Stigma around infertility**: Talking openly about infertility itself was much less common than it is now, and people often felt shame or embarrassment seeking treatment. IVF brought private struggles into the public eye.  
- **Novel science**: At the time, assisted reproductive technology was groundbreaking and poorly understood. Anything new involving reproduction or genetics tends to stir controversy until it becomes normalized.  

Over time, attitudes shifted. As IVF proved safe and more people benefited, it moved from taboo to mainstream. Today, millions of children worldwide have been born via IVF, and in many countries it’s widely accepted — though in some cultures and religious contexts, there are still sensitivities.
