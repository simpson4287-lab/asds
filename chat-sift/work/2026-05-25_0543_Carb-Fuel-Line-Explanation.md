[M2] AI (2026-05-25 05:43):
No, it is **probably not an air line**. On this type of small diaphragm carb, the two lines are usually:

**1. Fuel feed / pickup line**  
This comes from the fuel tank, normally with a little fuel filter on the end inside the tank. It supplies fuel to the carb.

**2. Primer return / purge line**  
This sends excess fuel and air bubbles back to the tank when you press the primer bulb. It is often called the **return line**, **purge line**, or **overflow return**.

So the primer system is not really “pumping fuel into the engine” directly; it usually **draws fuel through the carb and pushes the excess back to the tank**, removing air from the carb circuit.

From your photo, the two dangling transparent/brownish lines are very likely:

- one **from tank/filter to carb inlet**
- one **from carb/primer circuit back to tank**

A good check is:

Press the primer bulb. The line that has fuel visibly moving **back toward the tank** is the return line. The line that must stay connected to the tank filter is the feed line.

The tank vent/air breather is normally a **separate vent**, not one of the carb fuel nipples. So don’t leave one of those carb pipes open to air unless the manufacturer diagram specifically shows it.

1. **Take a photo first** of where both fuel lines go.
2. Remove the **two screws around the primer bulb cover**.
3. Lift off the metal cover and the bulb.
4. Check the bulb for splits, hardening, or pinholes. Replace it if it is stiff or cracked.

I’d be **very cautious with needles** on this carb.

For the **fuel hoses**, needles can be useful for cleaning the *inside of the pipe ends* or the brass hose nipples, but for the **tiny carb drillings/jets/check-valve holes**, don’t push a steel needle through them unless you’re basically accepting the risk of damaging it.

2. **Let it soak** for a few minutes.

5. For poking, a **soft copper wire strand**, nylon bristle, or plastic pick is safer than a steel needle.

For your photo, I’d clean the aluminium carb body holes with spray and air, clean the brass pipe stubs, then reassemble. If the primer bulb is hard, cloudy, cracked, or slow to spring back, replace the bulb/diaphragm kit rather than trying to “needle clean” it.

For small strimmer/chainsaw/blower diaphragm carbs like that, the fuel tube is **typically 3 mm OD × 2 mm ID**, sometimes sold as:

| Use | Typical size |

- brass nipple about **4 mm OD** → use **3 mm ID tube**

Buy **proper petrol-resistant fuel line**, ideally yellow/translucent **Tygon-style** tubing. Don’t use ordinary silicone tube; petrol will soften/swell it.

I’d buy a small mixed kit if unsure: **1.5×2.5, 2×3, and 3×5 mm**. It’s cheap and saves messing around.

Yes — exactly. With the **primer bulb fitted**, that side of the carb will often only pass fuel/cleaner in the intended direction because there are **tiny one-way check valves/flaps** in the primer circuit.

So if you’re trying to spray carb cleaner **backwards through that nipple**, it may refuse to go because the check valve is doing its job.

On this type of carb:

- One passage allows fuel/air to be **drawn through the carb** when the bulb springs back.
- Another passage lets fuel/air be **pushed out to the return line** when you press the bulb.
- The little rubber flaps/check valves stop it just sloshing backwards and forwards.

So don’t force it hard. If it won’t accept spray from that direction with the bulb assembled, that does **not automatically mean it’s blocked**.

The better test now is:

1. Put both lines in a little pot of fresh fuel or cleaner.
2. Press and release the primer bulb.
3. You should see it **draw fluid in on release** and **push fluid out on press**.
4. The bulb should spring back cleanly and not stay collapsed.

If the bulb only works one way, that is normal. If it won’t draw fuel at all, the issue is usually line routing, split fuel hose, hardened bulb, or damaged/dirty check-valve diaphragm.

[M20] AI (2026-05-25 09:21):
No — they **do not normally need to be the same length**.

On a small 2-stroke carb like this, the two hoses usually have different jobs and different lengths:

**Fuel pickup / intake line**  
This one should be the **longer line inside the tank**. It normally has the little fuel filter/weighted pickup on the end, and it must be long enough to lie at the lowest part of the tank so it stays submerged when the machine is tilted.

**Return / purge line**  
This one can be **shorter inside the tank**. It only dumps excess fuel/air back into the tank from the primer circuit. It does not need to reach the bottom.

So if you had to tilt the fuel toward one line to make it pump, that strongly suggests the **pickup line/filter end is too short, snapped, split, or not sitting in the fuel**.

For cutting new hose:

1. Fit the hose onto the carb nipples first — push it fully over the brass barb/bead.
2. For the **pickup line**, leave enough length inside the tank for the filter to sit on the bottom and move around a bit. It should not be stretched tight.
3. For the **return line**, it only needs to poke into the tank by maybe **10–30 mm**, enough that it returns fuel into the tank and does not pull out.
4. Outside the tank, keep both hoses long enough to reach the carb naturally with no kinks, but not so long that they loop sharply.

From your photo, the line that “barely enters the gasket” sounds like it may have **broken off at the tank/grommet or carb nipple end**. Replace both hoses while you’re there — old translucent brown hose goes hard and cracks, then the primer just sucks air.

A good rule: **long line = pickup/filter/feed to carb; short line = return from primer back to tank.**

[M26] AI (2026-05-25 09:25):
Yes — if it still doesn’t start after new fuel line/filter and the carb is passing fuel, the next checks are basically **spark, fuel delivery, air, compression, exhaust**. I’d do it in this order because it avoids chasing random faults.

### 1. Spark plug first

Remove the plug and inspect it.

A healthy plug should be dry-ish, light brown/grey on the electrode, and not physically damaged.

Replace it if it is:

- wet with fuel after repeated pulls
- black/oily/carboned up
- cracked porcelain
- rusty
- has a badly worn electrode

You can test spark by fitting the plug into the HT cap, holding the metal body of the plug against bare engine metal, and pulling the starter. You should see a clear blue/white spark. Don’t hold the metal plug body with your hand.

If there is no spark, then next suspect is the **kill switch wiring**, HT lead/cap, coil/module air gap, or ignition coil.

### 2. Confirm whether it’s fuel or ignition

This is the useful split:

Remove the air filter and give a **tiny** amount of fuel mix or carb cleaner into the intake, then pull it over.

- If it **fires briefly then dies**, ignition and compression are probably OK, and you still have a fuel/carb delivery problem.
- If it **does absolutely nothing**, suspect spark, compression, flooding, exhaust blockage, or timing/coil issue.

Use only a tiny amount. Don’t keep spraying lots in; that can flood it or wash oil off the cylinder.

### 3. Check primer and fuel routing

After refitting the new pipe, press the primer bulb. You should see fuel moving.

Normally:

- tank filter line feeds the carb
- primer/purge line returns fuel back to tank
- primer bulb should fill/purge air bubbles
- bulb should spring back quickly

If the bulb stays flat, you have a blocked pickup, wrong pipe routing, blocked tank vent, or stuck check valve.  
If it fills but the engine won’t keep running, the metering side of the carb may still have an issue.

### 4. Air filter

Check the air filter isn’t soaked in fuel/oil or completely clogged. For testing only, you can try starting briefly with the filter removed. Don’t run it properly without the filter, but it’s fine as a diagnostic.

### 5. Exhaust / spark arrestor screen

Very common on 2-strokes. The exhaust outlet or spark arrestor mesh can clog with carbon/oil.

Symptoms:

- starts but won’t rev
- starts then dies
- feels strangled
- plug gets wet/black
- lots of smoke

Remove the muffler cover or spark arrestor screen if accessible and clean the carbon. If badly blocked, it may not start at all.

### 6. Carb diaphragms / metering needle side

The side opposite the primer bulb usually has the **metering diaphragm**, needle, and lever. If the diaphragm has gone stiff, warped, or crispy, the carb may prime but not actually meter fuel when the engine is trying to run.

Serviceable parts there are:

- metering diaphragm
- gasket
- needle valve
- lever/spring
- inlet screen/filter inside the carb

Given your fuel line has gone hard/snapped, the carb rubber parts may also be aged. A diaphragm kit or full replacement carb may be easier than fighting it.

### 7. Compression

If it has spark and fuel but still won’t even cough, check compression.

Rough field test: pull the starter slowly. You should feel firm pulses/resistance. If it spins over very easily, compression may be low.

Causes:

- worn piston/ring
- scored cylinder
- leaking crank seals
- loose carb/manifold gasket
- loose spark plug

On small 2-strokes, crankcase sealing matters too, because the engine uses crankcase pulses to pump fuel and move mixture.

### 8. Tank vent

A blocked tank vent can stop fuel being drawn. Try starting with the fuel cap slightly loosened. If it suddenly behaves better, the vent is blocked.

For your case, I’d do: **new fuel line + filter → fresh 2-stroke mix → new/clean plug → primer flow test → tiny fuel-in-intake test**. That will quickly tell you whether you’re still on the fuel side or need to move to ignition/compression.

Yes, it may well be flooded now, especially if you’ve had fuel in the bulb and have been pulling it over while the hose/filter situation is suspect.

While waiting for the new hose, you can still do useful checks without needing it to actually run.

### 1. Clear a possible flood

Do this first:

1. Switch ignition to **OFF**.
2. Remove the spark plug.
3. Check the plug:
   - **wet with fuel** = flooded or over-fuelling
   - **bone dry** = probably not getting fuel into the engine
4. With plug removed, pull the starter **10–20 times** to blow excess fuel vapour out.
5. Leave the plug out for **10–20 minutes**.
6. Dry the plug with tissue, or gently warm it. Don’t use a naked flame.
7. Refit plug, throttle open, **no choke**, then try again.

For a flooded two-stroke, the usual restart attempt is:

**choke OFF, throttle open, pull several times.**

Don’t keep pressing the primer bulb during this. Once the bulb has fuel in it, more priming can just make the situation worse.

---

### 2. Check spark while the plug is out

With the plug removed:

1. Put the plug back into the HT cap.
2. Hold the metal thread/body of the plug firmly against bare engine metal.
3. Switch ignition **ON**.
4. Pull starter.

You want a visible blue/white spark at the plug gap.

No spark means don’t chase the carb yet — check kill switch, plug cap, lead, coil gap, or just try a new plug.

A new spark plug is a sensible cheap service item anyway.

---

### 3. Use the plug wet/dry result as a diagnostic

After a few start attempts:

**Wet plug:**  
Fuel is reaching the cylinder, but it isn’t firing. Likely flooding, weak/no spark, wrong choke procedure, blocked exhaust, or very rich carb.

**Dry plug:**  
Fuel is not reaching the cylinder. Likely pickup hose/filter, carb metering diaphragm, blocked jet, incorrect fuel line routing, air leak in hose, or primer circuit problem.

Given yours only worked when tilted and one hose looks snapped, a **dry plug** would not surprise me.

---

### 4. Do a tiny “will it fire?” test

Once you’ve cleared flooding and confirmed spark:

1. Remove air filter.
2. Put **one tiny drop** of fresh two-stroke mix into the carb intake, or a very short mist of carb cleaner.
3. Choke OFF.
4. Pull starter.

Result:

- **Fires briefly then dies** = ignition/compression are probably OK; fuel delivery/carb/hose is still the issue.
- **Does nothing at all** = check spark again, plug, flooding, compression, exhaust blockage.

Use very little. Don’t keep spraying loads in.

---

### 5. Check the air filter and exhaust screen

Because your original symptom was **started but cut out when revving**, also check:

- Air filter not soaked or blocked.
- Exhaust outlet / spark arrestor mesh not choked with oily carbon.
- Fuel cap vent not blocked.

A blocked spark arrestor can make it start but die when revved because it cannot breathe out.

---

### 6. Don’t judge the carb properly until the hoses are fixed

With a snapped/short pickup hose, the primer/carb can behave very misleadingly. It may pull air, intermittently pick up fuel, or only work when tilted.

So for now I’d do:

**clear flood → check spark → check plug wet/dry → tiny fuel-in-intake test → check exhaust/air filter.**

Then when the hose arrives, replace both lines and the in-tank filter before making a final judgement on the carb.
