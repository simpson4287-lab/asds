[M2] AI (2026-05-14 22:59):
That can absolutely happen in some organisations, but the important nuance is that it is usually a *cultural and leadership failure*, not something inherently tied to HR as a profession.

A healthy HR function should operate more like a governance and organisational support mechanism:
- consistent,
- procedurally fair,
- emotionally regulated,
- evidence-led,
- and professionally detached from interpersonal factions.

When HR becomes cliquey, dismissive, or socially exclusionary, a few patterns are often present:

- weak executive oversight,
- long-standing internal relationships overriding policy,
- informal social power structures,
- lack of accountability,
- defensive behaviour under organisational stress,
- or HR becoming overly aligned with management personalities rather than organisational process.

The “small office” effect is real as well. In tightly networked environments, people can unconsciously:
- protect familiar personalities,
- normalise poor behaviour,
- minimise concerns from quieter employees,
- or interpret professionalism through social compatibility rather than objective contribution.

One of the more difficult dynamics is that HR departments often control or influence:
- communication narratives,
- behavioural framing,
- appraisal language,
- wellbeing pathways,
- and procedural interpretation.

If that function becomes socially biased, employees can feel:
- subtly isolated,
- not believed,
- managed rather than supported,
- or judged through personality fit rather than measurable performance.

That said, it is also important not to generalise too broadly. Many HR professionals are highly competent, ethical, and quietly effective — especially in larger or more mature organisations with:
- strong governance,
- transparent process,
- calibrated appraisal systems,
- documented decision-making,
- and leadership that expects professionalism from HR itself.

A useful distinction is this:

| Healthy HR Culture | Dysfunctional HR Culture |
|---|---|
| Process-led | Personality-led |
| Consistent standards | Selective enforcement |
| Calm and evidence-based | Defensive and political |
| Confidential and respectful | Gossip-driven |
| Supports resolution | Protects internal image |
| Professionally detached | Socially tribal |

The challenge for employees is that dysfunctional HR behaviour is often *subtle rather than overt*. It may appear as:
- vague feedback,
- endless ambiguity,
- shifting standards,
- unexplained resistance,
- social coldness,
- selective responsiveness,
- or an overemphasis on “tone” and “communication style” without concrete operational examples.

Those experiences can be particularly difficult for technically focused employees, neurodivergent staff, or people who operate in a highly literal/evidence-driven way, because they expect:
- clarity,
- traceability,
- objective criteria,
- and proportionality.

When those things are absent, the environment can feel irrational and destabilising even if nobody is explicitly hostile.

The healthiest response is usually:
- staying factual,
- documenting clearly,
- separating emotion from evidence,
- avoiding escalation through frustration,
- and continually steering conversations back toward observable behaviours, deliverables, and agreed standards rather than personalities or social interpretation.
