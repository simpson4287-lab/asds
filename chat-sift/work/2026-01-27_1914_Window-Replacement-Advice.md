[M2] AI (2026-01-27 19:14):
Yes — **you can omit a supplied window and infill the opening with matching timber**, but **how** you do it matters.

### Correct approach (recommended)
- **Do not install the window frame at all.**
- Continue stacking **standard wall logs** through that position, exactly as if the window were never specified.
- Use logs of the **same profile** (45 mm wall logs with the same tongue-and-groove and corner notches).
- If required, cut logs to length using **spare wall logs** supplied in the pack (the manual explicitly allows cutting spares) fileciteturn0file0.

This results in a **structurally continuous wall** and preserves:
- log settlement behaviour  
- weather tightness  
- strength and rigidity  

### What *not* to do
- ❌ Do **not** fit a timber “panel” or sheet into a window aperture.
- ❌ Do **not** install the window frame and then board it over.
  
Window and door openings are designed to **float** to accommodate timber movement. Filling an aperture with fixed stock timber will almost certainly cause:
- gaps as the cabin settles  
- cracking or distortion  
- water ingress over time  

### Practical note
The Daft Badger manual confirms that **door and window layouts are optional and can be changed**, and that spare wall logs may be cut and reused where needed fileciteturn0file0. Treat the wall as a *no-window wall* from the outset and it behaves correctly.

If you want, you can later:
- cut in a window at a different height/location, or  
- leave the wall solid permanently (better acoustically and thermally).

[M4] AI (2026-01-27 19:23):
Short answer: **do not try to mechanically interlock two finished log walls using the corner overhang profiles.** It will work briefly, then cause binding, gaps, or distortion as the cabins move independently.

Below is the **best-practice approach** for two Daft Badger–style log cabins placed side-by-side.

---

## Why interlocking the overhangs is a bad idea
Each cabin is a **free-moving log stack**:
- Logs expand/contract with moisture
- Walls settle vertically over time
- Each cabin settles **slightly differently**

If you cut and mesh the corner overhang tongues (as sketched), you create:
- A **rigid shear lock** between two structures
- No allowance for differential settlement
- High risk of:
  - cracked logs  
  - forced gaps elsewhere  
  - doors/windows binding  
  - roof stress  

Manufacturers explicitly design these cabins to **move as a unit**, not be stitched together at log level.

---

## Correct structural strategy (recommended)

### 1. Treat the cabins as **two independent shells**
- Each cabin keeps:
  - its own wall stack
  - its own settlement behaviour
  - its own roof load path

No log-to-log interlocking between cabins.

---

### 2. Create a **separate linking structure**
Between the cabins, build a **non-load-bearing infill / corridor**:

**Typical stack (best practice):**
- Independent timber stud frame between cabins
- 10–25 mm movement gaps to *both* log walls
- Flexible membrane / compressible gasket against logs
- Internal trims that **slide**, not screw through logs
- External flashing or cover board (also floating)

This corridor:
- Can be insulated heavily
- Can carry doors/windows
- Can be airtight
- Does **not** interfere with log movement

---

### 3. If you want a “visually continuous” interior wall
Internally, you can:
- Leave a small gap (10–15 mm)
- Cover it with:
  - vertical cover boards
  - floating trims fixed to **one side only**
- Let the joint disappear visually without structural coupling

---

## What *can* be joined safely
- ✅ Roof flashing (floating detail)
- ✅ External cover trims (fixed one side only)
- ✅ Internal corridor framing
- ❌ Log tongues / corner overhangs
- ❌ Screwing logs from one cabin into the other

---

## If you insist on sharing alignment (advanced / risky)
Only acceptable method:
- **One cabin is designated “primary”**
- Second cabin wall is:
  - kept ~15 mm clear
  - laterally restrained with **slotted steel brackets**
  - vertical movement fully free

Even this is **non-standard** and not manufacturer-approved.

---

## Bottom line
Your sketch shows a **rigid log-to-log stitch**.  
That is the *one thing* log cabins are designed to avoid.

**Best advised solution:**  
> Two independent cabins + a floating, framed, insulated link corridor.

The gap is **not sealed with a rigid seal**.  
It is sealed with a **compressible + sliding system** that stays weather-tight while allowing movement.

---

## External weather seal (primary defence)

### 1. **EPDM or compressible foam strip**
- Fixed to **one cabin only**
- Compressed lightly against the other cabin
- Thickness: **10–20 mm**
- Acts as:
  - rain seal  
  - wind seal  
  - movement buffer  

Examples (conceptually):
- EPDM D-section / P-section
- Closed-cell PE / neoprene foam strip

**Key rule:**  
Nothing bridges rigidly from cabin A to cabin B.

---

### 2. **Cover board / flashing (floating)**
- Timber cover board or metal flashing
- Fixed to **one cabin only**
- Overlaps the gap by 30–50 mm
- Allows the other cabin to move behind it

This stops:
- driven rain
- UV degradation of the gasket

---

## Internal airtight / acoustic seal

### 3. **Secondary compressible seal**
On the inside face:
- Expanding foam tape **or**
- Backer rod + flexible acoustic sealant

This is **not structural**—it just stops:
- drafts
- sound leakage

Use:
- low-modulus sealant (MS polymer / acoustic sealant)
- never silicone between two moving log stacks

---

### 4. **Floating internal trim**
- Decorative board
- Fixed to **one side only**
- Covers the movement gap
- Slides over the other wall

---

## What not to use
- ❌ Expanding PU foam between cabins (locks movement)
- ❌ Mortar / filler / hard caulk
- ❌ Screws through both walls
- ❌ Silicone bridging two moving structures

---

## Typical gap sizes
- **External gap:** 10–15 mm minimum  
- **Seal compression:** 30–50% of foam thickness  
- **Cover overlap:** 30–50 mm

Below are the common forms, from simplest to most robust.

---

## 1. Flat timber cover board (most common)

**What it is**
- A straight plank
- Typical size: **18–22 mm thick × 70–120 mm wide**
- Runs vertically the full height of the wall

**How it’s fixed**
- Screwed to the **middle self-standing wall only**
- Overlaps the cabin logs by 30–50 mm
- No fixings into the cabins

**Why it works**
- Allows cabins to move behind it
- Easy to replace
- Visually clean and “timber correct” for a log cabin
