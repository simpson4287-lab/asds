### 🏛️ 1. Public vs publicly accessible land  
Even if a space is called “public” (like a park, nature reserve, or school field), it’s usually **publicly accessible land owned or managed by a local authority**. That doesn’t automatically mean you have a right to enter it at any time.  

If a council sign says **“Closed after 10 pm”** or **“No access outside opening hours”**, then going in after that time is treated as **unauthorised entry** — effectively **trespass**.  

### 🚷 2. Trespass
- Civil trespass: entering without permission (even with no damage or harm). Usually not a criminal offence, but staff, wardens, or police can ask you to leave and you can be removed with “reasonable force.”  
- Aggravated trespass (under the *Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994*): if you disrupt lawful activity (e.g. construction, maintenance, events), it becomes **a criminal matter**.  
- Some sites (railways, schools, MOD, utilities, etc.) are covered by **specific by-laws**, and being there when closed **is criminal trespass**.

### ⚖️ 3. Exceptions
You might have limited rights of access under:
- **The Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 (CROW)** for certain mapped “open access” land (moorland, downland, etc.), but even these areas can have **seasonal or safety restrictions** posted.
- **Public rights of way** (footpaths, bridleways) generally remain open 24 hours — but you must stay on the path and not stray into closed areas.  

### 🪧 4. Indicators that it’s closed
If there’s a **locked gate**, **signage stating opening hours**, **CCTV monitoring**, or **by-law notice**, then it’s legally considered **closed to the public** — and entering is trespass.
