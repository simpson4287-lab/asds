No — it’s **not agreed**. But it is a serious, plausible outcome.

A better wording would be:

**In an AGI/ASI world, money may become much less important for many goods, but not automatically worthless.**

Money only loses value if **scarcity collapses**. If ASI can produce energy, food, housing, medicine, design, software, labour and manufacturing at near-zero cost, then ordinary “earning money to buy necessities” could become far less central. OpenAI itself frames AGI as potentially increasing abundance and “turbocharging” the economy, while institutions like the IMF focus more cautiously on productivity gains, labour disruption and inequality rather than guaranteed post-scarcity. citeturn460332search9turn460332search1

The key issue is that **not everything becomes abundant just because intelligence becomes abundant**. Land, political power, legal rights, scarce materials, energy infrastructure, compute access, human attention, status, safety, and ownership of AI systems may still be scarce. In that world, money might still matter a lot — or be replaced by some other allocation system.

There are roughly three outcomes people discuss:

**1. Abundance outcome:** ASI makes most goods and services incredibly cheap. Money still exists, but basic survival no longer depends on wages.

**2. Inequality outcome:** ASI creates vast wealth, but it is owned by whoever controls the AI, compute, energy, robotics, land, IP, and institutions. In that case money may become *more* important for those excluded from ownership. The IMF and other economists emphasise this inequality risk. citeturn460332search1turn460332search7

**3. Post-labour / social-contract outcome:** human labour becomes economically weak, so wages lose their role as the main way people access resources. Then society may need UBI, public AI ownership, AI dividends, or some other redistribution mechanism. Some AGI economic models explicitly discuss human wages collapsing if AGI substitutes for labour at scale. citeturn460332academia64turn460332academia70

So the clean answer is:

**Yes, “money having little value” is one possible AGI/ASI scenario, but it is not the consensus. The real consensus is weaker: advanced AI could massively disrupt the relationship between labour, income, ownership, and scarcity.**

My own take: money probably would not vanish first. **Wages would become the vulnerable bit first.** Money may still exist, but many humans may no longer be able to earn it in the old way. That is the part that makes AGI/ASI economically radical.

[M5] AI (2026-06-08 19:17):
It could push black markets in **two opposite directions**, depending on how AGI/ASI changes scarcity, policing, and social conditions.

If material abundance improves and people have stable housing, healthcare, meaning, and support, then some demand for illicit drugs could fall. A lot of harmful drug use is linked to pain, trauma, boredom, poverty, isolation, untreated ADHD/depression, and lack of future. Remove enough of those drivers and the market weakens.

But AGI/ASI could also make black markets **more sophisticated**.

The big effects would likely be:

**Demand might change rather than disappear.**  
Even in a wealthy society, people still seek intoxication, escape, pleasure, status, novelty, rebellion, or self-medication. So the market may shrink for desperation-driven use, but remain for enhancement, recreation, or emotional escape.

**Synthetic production could become easier.**  
Advanced automation and AI-assisted chemistry could lower the barrier for making novel psychoactive substances. That does not mean everyone could safely do it, but criminal groups could become more agile, making new compounds faster than regulators can classify them.

**Law enforcement could become far more capable.**  
AI surveillance, pattern detection, financial tracing, package scanning, border analytics, and predictive policing could make conventional drug trafficking much harder. Street dealing and large supply chains might become easier to detect.

**The market could move from “substances” to “experiences.”**  
If future tech can directly alter mood, perception, reward, or consciousness through neurotechnology, virtual reality, pharmacology, or stimulation, the illicit market may shift away from traditional drugs toward unlicensed enhancement, black-market neural devices, illegal mood systems, or hacked therapeutic tools.

**Organised crime may pivot.**  
If money becomes less central, gangs may not vanish. They may move toward scarce goods: access to compute, restricted biotech, weapons, identity systems, private security, political leverage, or banned experiences.

The uncomfortable bit is this: **ASI could reduce the reasons people turn to destructive drug use, while also making illicit drug markets more technically capable.**

So the likely outcome is not “black markets disappear.” It is more like:

**Low-level street drug markets may weaken, but high-tech illicit markets could become more dangerous, personalised, and harder to regulate.**

The best-case AGI world reduces addiction by removing misery and improving treatment. The worst-case one gives organised crime better tools, while people still have pain they want to escape.

[M8] AI (2026-06-08 19:20):
If job losses were **individual or moderate**, banks would mostly do what they already do: forbearance first, repossession last.

In the UK, that means things like:

- temporary interest-only payments  
- extending the mortgage term  
- temporary payment deferrals  
- reduced-payment arrangements  
- capitalising arrears later  
- giving time to sell voluntarily  
- repossession only after the lender has gone through the proper arrears process

That is not banks being kind; it is partly commercial logic and partly regulation. The FCA’s mortgage rules require lenders to deal fairly with borrowers in payment difficulty, and the UK Mortgage Charter explicitly includes options such as switching to interest-only, extending the term, or temporary deferral depending on circumstances. citeturn786452search2 citeturn786452search1

But if AGI caused **mass job loss**, the situation changes from “borrower problem” to **systemic financial-stability problem**.

Banks would probably not want mass repossessions, because if they repossess too many homes at once, they crash house prices, realise losses on their own mortgage books, flood the market, and damage the wider economy. So their incentive would be to keep people in homes where possible, especially if the crisis looked structural rather than a normal recession.

The likely sequence would be:

**First: emergency forbearance.**  
Banks would offer payment holidays, interest-only periods, term extensions, arrears freezes, or negotiated reduced payments. We saw similar logic during Covid and again with the Mortgage Charter after rate rises. FCA data shows hundreds of thousands of UK mortgages have already used Charter-style payment reductions since 2023. citeturn786452search0

**Second: government intervention.**  
If job losses were large enough, government would almost certainly step in. That could mean mortgage guarantee schemes, income support, temporary public payment support, restrictions on repossessions, or some version of UBI / AI dividend if the cause really was permanent automation displacement.

**Third: central-bank and regulator action.**  
The Bank of England already stress-tests major banks against severe downturns and house-price falls; the purpose is to make sure banks can keep lending and absorb shocks rather than collapse. Recent Bank of England material says the UK banking system is strongly capitalised and able to support households and businesses even under much worse conditions. citeturn786452search4

**Fourth: restructuring of debt.**  
If the labour market genuinely changed permanently, traditional 25–35 year wage-backed mortgages might no longer make sense. You could see longer mortgage terms, state-backed refinancing, shared-equity conversions, rent-to-own models, mortgage-to-rent schemes, or partial debt write-downs in extreme cases.

The brutal bit is this: **banks cannot simply forgive everyone’s mortgage**, because mortgages are assets on bank balance sheets and are often funded through deposits, wholesale markets, covered bonds, and securitisation. If mortgage payments stopped at scale, it would threaten banks, pension funds, insurers, and the wider credit system.

So the real answer is:

**For a normal unemployment shock, banks manage arrears case by case. For AGI-scale unemployment, the state would have to become involved, because banks alone could not absorb mass non-payment without risking a housing and banking crisis.**

In an AGI/ASI world, the deeper question becomes: **if human wages stop being the main income source, what replaces them as the basis for housing finance?** Mortgages only work because lenders assume future income. If that assumption breaks, the whole housing-finance model has to be redesigned.

[M10] AI (2026-06-08 19:26):
Under **mass job loss from AGI/ASI**, the best and worst outcomes are basically separated by one thing:

**Do governments redesign the income/ownership system fast enough, or do they leave people exposed to wage collapse while debts remain fixed?**

## Best outcome

The best outcome is not “banks generously forgive everything.” It is more like a **managed transition**.

People lose conventional jobs, but society recognises quickly that the problem is structural, not personal failure. Government steps in with some combination of:

**UBI / AI dividend / public wealth fund**  
People receive income because AI-driven production is generating huge value, even if human labour is no longer the main source of that value.

**Mortgage forbearance and restructuring**  
Banks are pressured or required to offer long payment holidays, interest-only periods, term extensions, arrears freezes, or state-backed refinancing.

**No mass repossession policy**  
Government and regulators make clear that mass forced selling would damage everyone: households, banks, house prices, pensions, and the wider economy.

**Debt is slowly adapted to the new reality**  
Some mortgages might be converted into longer-term loans, shared-equity arrangements, state-backed loans, or rent-like models. The key is avoiding sudden default cascades.

**Housing becomes less tied to employment**  
This is the most important part. If AI makes society richer overall, housing access could shift away from “you must have a salary” toward citizen income, public housing, housing credits, or subsidised ownership.

In that best-case world, someone with a mortgage might experience something like:

> “My job disappeared, but payments were paused or reduced, I got transitional income, my lender restructured the mortgage, and I did not immediately lose my home.”

That would still be stressful, but not catastrophic.

## Worst outcome

The worst outcome is a **fixed-debt, collapsing-income crisis**.

People lose jobs, wages fall, new work is scarce, but mortgages remain due every month as if nothing has changed. Banks initially offer short-term help, but arrears build. Then:

**Defaults rise sharply**  
Large numbers of people cannot pay.

**Banks tighten lending**  
New mortgages become harder to get. Deposits required go up. Risk models assume unstable income.

**House prices fall**  
If repossessions and forced sales rise, more homes hit the market. Prices drop, especially in areas with high mortgage exposure.

**Negative equity appears**  
People owe more than their home is worth. They cannot sell without carrying debt forward.

**Repossession increases**  
Banks do not want mass repossessions, but if arrears become too large and no state rescue arrives, they start enforcing.

**The banking system comes under stress**  
Mortgages are assets. If many stop performing, banks, pension funds, insurers, and mortgage-backed investors take losses.

**Social unrest rises**  
If people see AI producing huge wealth while they lose homes, the legitimacy of the system starts to fracture.

In that worst-case world, the household experience is:

> “My job disappeared, no replacement income arrived, the bank gave temporary help, arrears mounted, the house fell in value, and I was eventually forced to sell or repossessed.”

That is the ugly scenario.

## Most likely outcome

The most likely outcome is probably **messy middle ground**.

Governments would not let the mortgage market simply implode if millions were affected. But they may react late, unevenly, and under political pressure.

So you could see:

- emergency mortgage holidays  
- temporary bans or limits on repossessions  
- state-backed refinancing  
- expanded benefits or UBI-lite schemes  
- pressure on banks to restructure  
- higher taxes on AI-owning firms  
- public anger over asset owners versus wage earners  
- big differences between homeowners, renters, mortgage-free owners, and landlords  

The real danger is timing. A six-month policy delay could wreck people even if a long-term solution eventually appears.

## Best vs worst in one line

**Best case:** AI makes society rich enough that income, mortgages, and housing are redesigned before people are ruined.

**Worst case:** AI destroys wages while debts, rents, and ownership structures remain unchanged.

The decisive question is not really “what would banks do?” It is:

**Who owns the AI wealth, and how quickly is it redistributed or converted into household income?**

Yes — **very possibly**, and the UK already has a version of that.

Right now, the **Rent a Room Scheme** lets you earn up to **£7,500 per year tax-free** from letting furnished accommodation in your **main home**. If someone else shares that income, the allowance is usually halved to **£3,750 each**. citeturn100945search1

So in a mass job-loss / mortgage-stress scenario, government could absolutely choose to expand that sort of relief. For example, they might:

- raise the tax-free threshold from £7,500 to something higher  
- temporarily exempt lodger income from tax during a crisis  
- allow more generous rules for homeowners at risk of arrears  
- offer council-tax incentives for taking in lodgers  
- create a “mortgage support through lodger income” scheme  
- make it easier for lenders to count lodger income in affordability assessments  

That would be politically quite attractive because it does **three things at once**:

**It helps homeowners pay mortgages.**  
A lodger paying £600–£900/month could be the difference between arrears and survival.

**It increases housing supply without building anything.**  
Spare rooms become usable housing quickly.

**It is cheaper for government than paying everyone’s mortgage directly.**  
Instead of the state writing huge cheques, it lets households generate income from underused space.

The obvious policy move would be to increase the Rent a Room allowance, because the current £7,500 limit equates to about **£625/month**, and in many areas that is now below actual room rents. GOV.UK still lists the threshold as £7,500, and recent commentary has argued that the limit has become outdated as rents have risen. citeturn100945search1 citeturn100945news34

For your kind of thinking — using the home more flexibly — this is one of the most realistic “soft landing” tools. It would not solve everything, but it could keep a lot of people out of arrears.

The catch is that it only works well where people have:

**a spare room, a safe home layout, lender/insurance permission, and the temperament to share their space.**

But yes, under severe economic stress, **lower tax on renting a room is exactly the kind of measure government might use**. It is already administratively simple, familiar, and less radical than mortgage forgiveness or universal basic income.

[M14] AI (2026-06-08 19:38):
For the **Rent a Room Scheme**, once your gross lodger income goes above **£7,500/year**, you usually have two choices:

## Option 1: Use Rent a Room relief

You pay income tax only on the amount **above £7,500**.

Example:

| Annual lodger income | Taxable amount |
|---:|---:|
| £7,500 | £0 |
| £9,000 | £1,500 |
| £12,000 | £4,500 |

That taxable amount is added to your other income and taxed at your normal income tax rate. So if you are a basic-rate taxpayer, it would usually be **20%**; higher-rate, **40%**; additional-rate, **45%**. GOV.UK confirms the £7,500 threshold and that it is halved to £3,750 if the income is shared. citeturn909356search0

So, roughly:

- £9,000 rent income → £1,500 taxable → **£300 tax at 20%**, or **£600 at 40%**
- £12,000 rent income → £4,500 taxable → **£900 tax at 20%**, or **£1,800 at 40%**

## Option 2: Opt out and use normal property income rules

Instead of taking the £7,500 Rent a Room relief, you can elect to be taxed on:

**rental income minus allowable expenses**

That might be better if your costs are high, but for a simple lodger arrangement, Rent a Room relief is often cleaner and better.

Important detail: the £7,500 is usually based on **gross receipts**, not profit. So if the lodger pays you rent plus contributions for utilities, meals, cleaning, etc., those can count towards the £7,500 limit. HMRC’s helpsheet says the annual limit applies to receipts from letting accommodation in your main home. citeturn909356search2

So the practical answer is:

**Above £7,500, the excess is taxed like normal income, unless you opt out and calculate actual rental profit instead.**
