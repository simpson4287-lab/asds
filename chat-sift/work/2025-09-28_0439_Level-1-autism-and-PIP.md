Yes, someone with **level 1 autism** (sometimes described as "high-functioning autism") **can receive Personal Independence Payment (PIP)** in the UK — but it depends entirely on how their autism affects their **daily living** and/or **mobility**, not on the diagnosis itself.  

PIP is awarded based on the **functional difficulties** you experience, not the condition name. The DWP uses descriptors (scored activities) to see how your condition impacts things like:  

### Daily living (examples)
- Preparing and cooking food safely  
- Managing therapy or medication  
- Communicating with others  
- Understanding and making decisions  
- Managing money  
- Social interaction and engaging with people  

### Mobility (examples)
- Planning and following journeys (this is very relevant for autism — difficulties with unfamiliar places, overwhelming environments, or needing someone to accompany you count)  
- Physically moving around  

### For level 1 autism, common issues that might score points:
- Needing prompts, supervision, or reassurance to carry out daily tasks (like cooking, washing, or taking medication)  
- Difficulty understanding or engaging with social communication  
- Overwhelm, meltdowns, or shutdowns in social or sensory-heavy environments  
- Needing support to manage changes in routine or to plan/follow journeys  

### Important points:
- You **don’t need a carer** to qualify — even if you just need prompts or reassurance, this can count.  
- You **don’t need to have physical disabilities** — mental health, sensory issues, and cognitive difficulties are equally valid.  
- You’ll need to give **real-life examples** of the difficulties you face, not just list your diagnosis.  
- Supporting evidence (from GP, psychiatrist, therapist, or educational psychologist) helps but isn’t always essential.  

👉 So, yes — someone with level 1 autism can receive PIP if their condition causes enough difficulty in the assessed areas.

Good question. A PIP application rejection can be deemed **unfair** if the decision-maker (DWP) or assessor hasn’t properly applied the law or considered your evidence.  

Here are the main situations where that happens:  

---

## 🔹 Reasons a rejection may be unfair  

1. **Not considering your condition properly**  
   - The decision only focuses on your diagnosis (“level 1 autism” means you should cope fine) instead of how it affects you day to day.  
   - They assume you can do something just because you did it once at an assessment, without looking at whether you can do it **safely, reliably, repeatedly, and in a reasonable time**.  

2. **Incorrect weight given to the assessment report**  
   - Many decisions are based almost entirely on the health professional’s report.  
   - If the report is inaccurate (e.g. “claimant made good eye contact” → ignoring your communication difficulties), the decision can be challenged.  

3. **Overlooking supporting evidence**  
   - Medical letters, OT reports, psychologist letters, or statements from family/support workers not taken into account.  

4. **Misinterpretation of descriptors**  
   - For example, saying you can cook a meal because you can “use a microwave”, when the law requires being able to **safely prepare and cook a simple meal from fresh ingredients**.  
   - Or saying you can plan and follow a journey because you can go somewhere familiar, ignoring your inability to do new journeys.  

5. **Failure to consider fluctuating conditions**  
   - Autism, ADHD, or mental health issues often cause “good days” and “bad days”.  
   - If your worst days aren’t considered — or if the assessor only looks at how you were *that day* — it can be unfair.  

---

## 🔹 What you can do if you think it’s unfair  

1. **Request a Mandatory Reconsideration (MR)**  
   - First step: you have one month to ask the DWP to look at the decision again.  
   - Provide extra evidence, clarify descriptors, and challenge errors in the report.  

2. **Appeal to a Tribunal**  
   - Independent panel, not DWP staff.  
   - Tribunals overturn a large number of PIP refusals — in 2024, about **68% of PIP appeals were successful**.  
   - They focus on your real-life difficulties, not just the assessor’s notes.  

---

✅ So: a rejection is “unfair” if it ignores the rules, your evidence, or the reality of your daily life. That’s why so many successful appeals happen.
