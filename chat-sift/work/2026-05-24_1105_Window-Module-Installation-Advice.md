[M2] AI (2026-05-24 11:05):
Yes — structurally that is usually quite achievable on a 44 mm interlocking log cabin, *provided you treat the two windows as one combined opening* rather than simply “adding another hole”.

Your sketch is actually the right instinct: the key is creating a properly framed aperture that carries the wall loads around the wider opening.

A typical approach would be:

- remove the existing window module
- cut/open the wall for the second aperture
- create a continuous framed opening
- install both window units side-by-side
- tie them together mechanically
- add trim/cover battens internally and externally

The important part is the framing strategy.

### What’s really happening structurally

Those interlocking 44 mm wall logs normally contribute to:
- lateral stiffness
- wall alignment
- roof load transfer down to corners

When you widen the opening, you lose some of that interlock strength in the centre section.

So you normally add:
- a proper timber header/lintel above
- vertical studs/jambs at sides
- a joining mullion between the windows
- screw-fixed reinforcement plates/brackets

Something roughly like:

- Side stud
- Window 1
- Central reinforced mullion
- Window 2
- Side stud

rather than two loose window frames floating in the logs.

---

### The central mullion matters most

Because you already have a spare matching window, the cleanest method is often:

- bolt/screw the two window frames together first
- use packers to align them
- create one rigid “double-window cassette”
- fit that into one framed opening

The central join then acts like a structural mullion.

If the manufacturer’s frames are reasonably chunky softwood, this can work very well.

You may still want:
- concealed flat steel brackets
- long timber screws
- additional timber between the frames

especially because cabin walls move seasonally.

---

### The big thing people forget: cabin movement

Log cabins expand/shrink vertically with moisture.

That means:
- you should NOT rigidly pin the wall logs to the window frame everywhere
- you need allowance above the frame
- many cabins use slip joints or compression gaps

If you hard-fix everything tightly:
- the roof/walls can load the windows
- frames can rack
- glazing can bind or crack
- doors stop shutting nicely

So above the combined window:
- leave a compression gap
- hide it with trim
- use slotted fixings where appropriate

Very important.

---

### Your proportions actually look reasonable

From your sketch:
- the remaining corner sections still look fairly wide
- window height is moderate
- roof span is not enormous (~3 m)

So this is not immediately screaming “bad idea”.

If this were:
- full-width glazing
- near corner cuts
- giant bifold openings

then I’d be more cautious.

---

### What I would personally do

For a neat and robust solution:

#### Frame
Use something like:
- 44×70 or 45×95 timber framing internally around the opening

#### Header
Add:
- continuous timber lintel/header above both windows

#### Centre
Between windows:
- doubled timber stud or engineered mullion
- screw both frames into it

#### Fixings
Use:
- long structural screws
- angle brackets concealed internally

#### Movement allowance
Leave:
- ~10–20 mm compression allowance above frame depending on cabin guidance

#### Finish
Then:
- internal architrave
- external cover trims
- breathable sealing tape or polymer sealant

and it’ll probably look factory.

---

One caveat:
some cheaper cabins rely heavily on uninterrupted log interlock for rigidity. If yours is lightweight or very flexible, you may notice more wall movement afterward unless the new framing is substantial enough.

If you want, I can also sketch:
- the proper timber framing layout,
- where the lintel goes,
- how the mullion should work,
- and how to preserve wall movement clearance

because that’s the bit that makes or breaks these modifications.
