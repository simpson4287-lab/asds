1. Introduction
Looking back from adulthood, I can now recognise a consistent pattern of experiences and behaviours throughout my childhood that align with characteristics commonly associated with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). At the time, these experiences were not understood as neurodevelopmental differences but were instead seen as quirks, sensitivities, or emotional difficulties. Reflecting as an adult, it’s clear that my early life was shaped by differences in sensory processing, emotional regulation, and social understanding that made the world feel unpredictable and often overwhelming.
2. Social and Emotional Processing
2.1 Early Social Awareness and Friendships
From a young age, I struggled to form and maintain friendships in the same way as my peers. I often preferred solitary play or spending time with animals rather than other children. At playgroup, I remember being coaxed by other children into climbing inside a large barrel. Once inside, they rolled it around roughly while laughing, leaving me trapped and terrified until an adult intervened. That moment stands out as one of my first experiences of social vulnerability — I didn’t understand that I was being mocked or manipulated, and it left a lasting sense of fear and confusion. My parents later tried to help me socialise by enrolling me in a Beavers group, but it became a weekly ordeal. The noise, unpredictability, and social expectations overwhelmed me to the point of meltdowns. Eventually, they stopped taking me as the emotional strain became too great. Looking back, I can now see these reactions as the result of sensory and social overload rather than stubbornness or defiance.
2.2 Emotional Expression and Regulation
Emotional expression in childhood was difficult for me. I often didn’t know what I was feeling until long after the moment had passed. When upset or overwhelmed, I would either cry uncontrollably or withdraw completely into silence. These shutdowns were frequently misinterpreted as defiance or moodiness. In reality, they were the result of emotional overload — my way of coping with too much sensory and emotional input. Writing or being alone in a quiet space was often the only way I could recover.
2.3 Early Social Rules and Interactional Awareness
Social rules and cues were confusing from the beginning. I often missed signals about when to speak, when to stop, or how to join in conversations. Interactions that came naturally to others required conscious effort for me. I would mimic behaviours that seemed to work for other children, without truly understanding why. This early form of masking helped me fit in briefly but was exhausting and unsustainable. The result was frequent social exhaustion and retreat into solitude.

At around thirteen, I experienced an incident that demonstrated my social naïvety and vulnerability. I liked a girl at school but didn’t know how to approach her. Another boy convinced me to take her phone as part of a so-called plan to get her attention. I trusted him, but he later stole the phone himself and threatened me into silence. I was manipulated and blamed, left feeling frightened and ashamed. In hindsight, this reflected both my naïve trust in others and my difficulty recognising manipulative intent — traits commonly seen in autistic individuals.
2.4 Exclusion and Self-Esteem Development
Being consistently overlooked and excluded — especially in school sports — deeply affected my self-esteem. Each experience of being left out reinforced a belief that I wasn’t good enough or capable of belonging. What I couldn’t express then was that I simply processed information and social situations differently. Without recognition or support, I internalised these experiences as personal failures. This early erosion of self-worth planted the seeds of low confidence that persisted into adolescence and adulthood.
3. Sensory and Physical Development
3.1 Comfort Objects and Sensory Regulation
Between the ages of three and four, I developed a strong attachment to a comfort blanket that became essential to my sense of safety and calm. I carried it almost everywhere and relied on it during moments of anxiety, overstimulation, or uncertainty. The soft texture provided comfort and predictability in a world that often felt too intense. When the blanket was lost at the local library, I experienced what I now recognise as a severe emotional meltdown. I was inconsolable for days and struggled to sleep or feel settled. At the time, this reaction seemed extreme to adults, but looking back, it was my system’s response to losing a vital sensory anchor.
3.2 Early Interoception and Bedwetting
Bedwetting continued until around age eight and was eventually treated with a loud alarm that trained my body to wake up. This difficulty wasn’t behavioural; it stemmed from delayed interoceptive awareness — my brain simply didn’t register internal bodily signals until it was too late. In hindsight, this fits with broader autistic traits involving differences in sensory and neurological arousal. It also reflects a lifelong pattern of being disconnected from internal cues such as hunger, tiredness, and emotional states.
3.3 Sensory Sensitivities and Overload
My sensory experiences as a child were heightened and often overwhelming. Sudden noises like barking dogs, sirens, or motorbikes would make me cover my ears or cry. Even small, repetitive sounds such as a ticking clock or buzzing light could capture my full attention and cause distress. Visually, I noticed every inconsistency — uneven patterns, crooked pictures, flickering lights — details that others seemed unaware of. These sensitivities often led to frustration or emotional outbursts, as I lacked the language to explain what was wrong.
3.4 Early Speech and Cognitive Development
Speech development was delayed — I spoke very little until around the age of three. However, once speech emerged, it advanced rapidly. I quickly began using complex words and phrases for my age, showing an unusual verbal intelligence despite earlier silence. This uneven developmental pattern is common in autistic children, where advanced abilities in one area contrast with delays in another. It also contributed to misunderstandings, as adults often assumed emotional maturity matched verbal ability, when in reality the two were far out of sync.
4. Cognitive and Behavioural Patterns
4.1 Early Rigidity and Rule Dependence
From a young age, I showed a strong preference for routines and clear rules. Predictability made me feel safe. I developed rituals that had to be followed precisely, such as watching 'The Crystal Maze' on television every week while eating a bowl of custard. This combination became a sensory and emotional comfort, and any change to that pattern caused significant distress. When the show stopped airing, it felt like a major crisis that took weeks to adjust to. Looking back, it was a clear example of my dependence on routine and sensory consistency to maintain emotional regulation.
4.2 Attention to Detail and Hyperfocus
Even as a child, I paid intense attention to small details — noticing things that others missed. Whether it was the texture of fabrics, tiny patterns on walls, or sounds in the background, my focus would narrow until the outside world faded away. At around nine years old, I discovered fishing at an open day with my father and was instantly captivated. I immersed myself in learning everything I could about it — reading manuals, studying methods, and practising alone at the local pond for hours. It became both a passion and a form of solitary comfort, combining my love of calm environments with deep, focused learning.

In my preteen years, I became equally absorbed by online chess. I enjoyed the logic, order, and predictability it offered — a structured environment free from the ambiguity of social interaction. These focused interests provided both intellectual stimulation and emotional safety, reflecting the pattern of hyperfocus that would continue throughout my life.
4.3 Early Signs of Masking and Camouflage
By primary school, I had begun masking instinctively — copying the speech and behaviour of others to blend in. I observed how classmates interacted and tried to mirror them, but it never felt natural. While this helped me avoid attention, it also created a sense of separation from my authentic self. Even then, I experienced exhaustion from trying to act 'normal,' though I didn’t yet understand why.
5. Routine, Structure, and Predictability
Routine was my anchor throughout childhood. I felt safest when days followed a predictable pattern and events unfolded as expected. Changes to plans, school schedules, or family routines caused significant distress. I would try to regain control by recreating order — rewriting lists, tidying spaces, or repeating actions that made me feel calm. Predictability provided emotional safety in an otherwise uncertain world.

6. Summary Reflection Summary

Looking back from adulthood, my childhood experiences reveal a consistent pattern of autistic traits across sensory, emotional, and social domains. I displayed heightened sensitivity to sensory input, a strong attachment to comforting textures, delayed interoceptive awareness, and difficulty interpreting social dynamics. I also relied heavily on familiar routines and rituals — such as the 'Crystal Maze and custard' routine — to create a sense of control and comfort. Emotional expression was limited by confusion and overload, and I preferred solitary, predictable environments such as fishing and online chess, where I could engage deeply without social pressure.

The exclusion I experienced in social and physical activities, particularly in sports and peer relationships, had a lasting effect on self-esteem. I was vulnerable to manipulation and misunderstanding due to my trusting nature and lack of awareness of others’ motives. These experiences were not the result of disinterest or defiance but of a brain that processed the world differently — one that sought predictability, fairness, and sensory balance. Although misunderstood at the time, these traits now make sense as part of an autistic profile that shaped both my challenges and the ways I learned to cope.

In hindsight, understanding these early experiences brings compassion to my younger self and clarity to the lifelong patterns that followed. These observations, viewed retrospectively, are consistent with a presentation of Autism Spectrum Disorder in early childhood and align with the characteristics recognised in later life.
