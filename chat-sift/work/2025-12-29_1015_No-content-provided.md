In this context, **“interlock”** refers to the **knit construction of the fabric**, not a feature or fastening of the garment.

### What an interlock knit is
image_group{"query":["interlock knit fabric close up","interlock knit structure diagram","polo shirt interlock cotton fabric"]}

An **interlock knit** is a **double-knit** structure made on two sets of needles that interlock with each other. Compared with standard single jersey (T-shirt fabric), it has several defining characteristics:

- **Double-faced and identical on both sides**  
  There is no obvious “right” or “wrong” side.

- **Thicker and denser**  
  Heavier, more substantial hand-feel than piqué or jersey.

- **Smoother surface**  
  Cleaner, more refined appearance—well suited to smart polos.

- **Better shape retention**  
  Resists twisting, curling at edges, and bagging over time.

- **Softer stretch**  
  Stretch comes mainly from the knit structure rather than elastane, so it feels controlled rather than elastic.

### Why brands use interlock for polos
For a garment like this long-sleeve polo, interlock cotton is chosen because it:

- Looks **sharper and more formal** than piqué
- Drapes cleanly under jackets or knitwear
- Feels **warmer and more substantial**, suitable for cooler weather
- Holds a **premium silhouette** after repeated wear and washing

### In plain terms
Calling it an **“interlock polo”** means:
> *This is a smooth, dense, double-knit cotton polo that sits smarter and feels more substantial than a typical polo shirt.*

[M5] AI (2025-12-29 10:20):
For **dyeing tan chinos**, the *best approach depends on whether you want to go darker, richer, or change colour entirely*. Tan is a forgiving starting point, but there are limits.

---

## First: check the fabric (critical)
Look at the care label.

- **100% cotton or ≥95% cotton** → ideal, predictable results  
- **Cotton with elastane (1–3%)** → fine, but expect slightly lighter seams/stretch areas  
- **Polyester blend (>20%)** → difficult; colour will be weak or uneven

If they’re typical cotton chinos, you’re in good territory.

---

## Best dye method (home, reliable)

### Use a **fibre-reactive dye** (best option)
Examples: Procion MX–type dyes (or Dylon Hand Dye in the UK).

**Why this is best**
- Bonds chemically with cotton
- Strong, even colour
- Wash-fast and long-lasting
- Predictable over tan

---

## Colour recommendations (tan → better results)

Tan already has warmth, so these work best:

### Safest / most successful
- **Navy**
- **Dark olive**
- **Chocolate brown**
- **Charcoal**
- **Black**

### Possible but less predictable
- Burgundy
- Forest green
- Rust

### Not recommended
- Light colours (stone, beige, cream)
- Cool greys
- Pastels  
You cannot dye lighter than tan.

---

## Practical step-by-step (condensed)

1. **Wash chinos** (no fabric softener)
2. **Dissolve dye fully** in hot water
3. **Submerge chinos** completely, keep moving constantly (10–20 min)
4. **Add salt/fixative** as instructed
5. **Rinse until clear**
6. **Wash separately**, air dry first time

Constant agitation is the difference between “good” and “patchy”.

---

## Professional alternative (best finish)
If they’re good chinos:
- Use a **local dry cleaner that offers garment dyeing**
- More even colour
- Better seam consistency
- No shrinkage surprises

---

## Expect these changes (normal)
- Stitching may stay slightly lighter
- Waistband/interfacing may dye differently
- Fabric may feel slightly firmer initially (softens after wash)

---

### Bottom line
If they’re cotton chinos, **dye darker, not different**.  
For home dyeing: **fibre-reactive dye + dark colour + constant agitation** is the winning formula.

Understood. If the goal is **restoration rather than recolouring**, you should **not dye the entire garment** unless absolutely necessary. Localised fading on chinos can usually be corrected with a **controlled refresh**, preserving the original shade and finish.

Below is the **lowest-risk, most accurate approach**, in order of preference.

---

## Best option: gentle, whole-garment colour refresh (most consistent)

Even if only a couple of areas are faded, the **most reliable way to make them disappear** is a **very mild overall dye bath** in the *same* tan tone.

### Why this works
- Spot dyeing almost always leaves halos or tide marks
- A weak bath evens everything out without darkening noticeably
- Maintains factory look if done correctly

### What to use
- **Dylon Hand Dye – “Camel”, “Sand”, or “Taupe”** (UK-available)
- Use **¼ to ⅓ of the packet only**

### Method (important details)
1. Wash chinos first (no conditioner)
2. Dissolve dye fully in hot water
3. Add chinos and agitate constantly
4. **Check colour at 3–5 minutes**  
   Remove early — you are refreshing, not recolouring
5. Rinse thoroughly, wash once separately

This typically restores faded seat/knees without obvious colour change.

---

## Targeted option: localised repair (higher skill, higher risk)

Only do this if the faded areas are small and discrete.

### What works best
- **Fabric dye applied with a sponge**, not a brush
- Heavily diluted dye (test on inside seam)
- Feather edges outward, never stop abruptly

### What not to do
- Pens or markers (always obvious)
- Strong dye straight from bottle
- Heat-setting before you’re sure it matches

This is useful for **belt-line fade or knee creases**, but it’s easy to overdo.

---

## Lowest-risk professional fix
If the chinos are good quality and well-fitted:

- Ask a **dry cleaner offering colour rebalancing / garment refreshing**
- Cost is modest compared to replacement
- Zero DIY risk

---

## Things to avoid (these make it worse)
- Sun drying (locks in uneven fade)
- Over-washing before fixing
- “Black tea / coffee” tricks (unreliable, smells, stains unevenly)
