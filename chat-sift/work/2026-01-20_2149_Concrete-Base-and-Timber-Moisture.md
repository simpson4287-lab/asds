[M2] AI (2026-01-20 21:49):
Short answer: **not if it’s built properly**. Long answer: timber hates sitting in damp limbo, so the floor build-up is all about breaking the paths that water and moisture would otherwise use to sneak in.

Here’s the physics of it, minus the sales brochure fluff.

### What actually makes timber “get wet”
There are three villains:
1. **Liquid water** (rain or groundwater moving sideways)
2. **Capillary rise** (water wicking upward through concrete)
3. **Water vapour** (moist air condensing under a cold floor)

A concrete slab by itself does *nothing* to stop these. It’s a sponge unless you tell it not to be.

### The correct stack-up (from concrete upward)
image_group{"query":["log cabin floor build up concrete base","timber floor on concrete slab damp proof membrane","cabin floor joists on concrete slab detail"]}

1. **Concrete slab with fall and drainage**
   - The slab should be higher than surrounding ground.
   - Ground around it slopes *away*.
   - Standing water next to the slab is the real enemy.

2. **Damp Proof Membrane (DPM)**
   - This is non-negotiable.
   - 1200-gauge polythene or similar, laid over the slab.
   - It stops capillary rise dead. Concrete can be wet underneath forever; the timber above never knows.

3. **Raised timber bearers / joists**
   - Timber is *not* laid flat on the slab.
   - Either:
     - Treated bearers on plastic shims, **or**
     - Joists sitting on DPC strips, **or**
     - Adjustable plastic supports (common in better cabins)
   - This creates an **air gap**, which is critical.

4. **Ventilation (even minimal)**
   - A small void lets vapour disperse instead of condense.
   - Trapped air = condensation = rot over time.

5. **Insulation goes *between* joists**
   - PIR boards or mineral wool.
   - Insulation without a vapour strategy underneath is how people accidentally grow mushrooms.

6. **Floor deck**
   - T&G boards or structural sheet.
   - Sealed on the warm side.

### The key misconception
> “If water gets under the slab, won’t the wood get wet?”

Only if:
- There’s no DPM, **or**
- Timber is touching concrete, **or**
- Water is pooling above slab level, **or**
- Moist air is trapped with nowhere to go

Water under concrete is normal. Timber touching wet concrete is the mistake.

### What *not* to do (very common DIY errors)
- Joists bolted directly to bare concrete  
- No DPM because “it’s a shed”  
- Slab flush with surrounding soil  
- Insulation laid straight on concrete  
- No airflow and no vapour control

Those failures don’t show up in year one. They show up in year five when the floor smells like a damp violin case.

### Bottom line
A concrete base is actually **one of the safest foundations** for a log cabin **if** you:
- isolate timber from concrete,
- block capillary moisture,
- and allow a little breathing room.

Do that, and the wood stays boringly dry for decades—which is exactly what timber wants.

- The **DPM below** stops ground moisture.
- The **floor deck above** acts as the warm-side boundary.

Do **not**:
- Lay insulation flat on the concrete
- Leave it touching wet surfaces
- Seal it airtight on both sides

That turns insulation into a damp sponge with ambition.

Insulating between the joists before laying the floor isn’t just allowed — it’s **the intended design**.  
You’re building a thermal sandwich: structure holds it, insulation slows heat, membranes control moisture. When each layer does only its job, nothing rots, nothing smells, and nothing needs revisiting.

That’s not overengineering. That’s just respecting how heat and water behave when no one’s watching.
