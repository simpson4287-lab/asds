Good question. In England & Wales, if the police are arranging a **voluntary interview under caution** (“Caution Plus 3”), they don’t normally tell a solicitor *exactly* what the eventual charge might be — because the point of the interview is to gather evidence before deciding.  

That said, there are some key bases on which the police would notify or involve a solicitor *before* the interview:  

1. **Right to legal advice**  
   - Anyone invited to a voluntary interview must be told they have the right to free legal advice.  
   - If the person asks for a solicitor, the police will usually provide a “disclosure” (at least a summary of the allegations) to that solicitor before the interview.  

2. **Pre-charge advice from CPS**  
   - In more serious or legally complex cases, the police may seek “early investigative advice” from the Crown Prosecution Service (CPS).  
   - If CPS has already indicated that there’s enough evidence to charge, the police may effectively be inviting the person to interview knowing a charge is highly likely.  

3. **Strength of evidence already gathered**  
   - If police have significant evidence (e.g. CCTV, witnesses, forensic results), they might already be leaning towards charge.  
   - In that case, disclosure to the solicitor prior to interview could reflect that position (though they usually present it as “allegations” rather than “we are charging”).  

4. **Safeguarding & procedural fairness**  
   - If the offence is serious (sexual offences, violence, vulnerable victims), the police are expected to ensure the suspect can have proper representation and that the solicitor knows the gist of the evidence in advance.  

5. **Professional conduct rules**  
   - Police are not supposed to mislead. If they *already know* a charge will be made (for example, CPS has authorised charge and interview is only to record the suspect’s account), then a solicitor should be told that. Otherwise, the interview could be challenged later as unfair.  

👉 In practice:  
- A solicitor will often only be told “the nature of the allegations” before interview, not whether a charge is inevitable.  
- If CPS has already directed charge, the solicitor must be informed because the interview then becomes more a matter of procedure than fact-finding.

# ⚖️ Adverse Inferences (UK, Criminal Cases)

Under the **Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994 (sections 34–37)**, if a suspect stays silent in interview, the court or jury may be invited to draw an **“adverse inference”** from that silence.  

---

## 🔹 1. Section 34 – Failing to Mention Facts Later Relied On
- If a suspect stays silent in interview but later, at trial, puts forward a defence (e.g. *“I was elsewhere at the time”*), the prosecution can argue:  
  > “If that were true, why didn’t you say it when first asked?”  
- The jury/judge may draw the inference that the defence was invented later.  

👉 This is the most common and most risky type of adverse inference.  

---

## 🔹 2. Section 35 – Silence at Trial
- If the accused refuses to testify at their trial, the court may also draw adverse inferences.  
- It doesn’t prove guilt on its own, but it can strengthen the prosecution’s case.  

---

## 🔹 3. Sections 36–37 – Failure to Account
- If arrested and found with suspicious objects, marks, or substances, and the suspect refuses to explain them, inferences may be drawn.  
- Similarly, if found at a place at a time linked to an offence and no explanation is given.  

---

## 🔹 Practical Effect
- **Silence alone cannot convict** — but combined with other evidence, it can tip the balance.  
- Judges direct juries that they *may* draw inferences, not that they *must*.  

---

## 🔹 Why Solicitors Still Advise “No Comment”
1. **Thin disclosure** – if the police haven’t revealed the case, it’s safer not to answer.  
2. **Weak evidence** – if the prosecution is shaky, staying silent stops the client accidentally filling gaps.  
3. **Consistency risks** – some clients panic or contradict themselves, which is worse than silence.  

To mitigate adverse inferences:  
- **Prepared statements** are often used.  
  - Defence is placed “on record” at interview.  
  - Prevents an inference that it was invented later.  
  - Client then says “no comment” to questioning.  

---

⚖️ **Summary**:  
- Staying silent *can* hurt later (adverse inference), but talking can hurt more if disclosure is weak.  
- Prepared statements give the best of both worlds: defence is noted, but client avoids cross-exam in police station.

# ⚖️ Drug Driving Charges  

## 🔹 1. Section 5A – “Over the Limit” (Specified Drug)  
**The law:**  
- It’s an offence to drive (or be in charge of a vehicle) with a specified controlled drug in your blood above the legal limit.  
- The prosecution only needs to prove:  
  1. You drove, and  
  2. The blood test result exceeded the legal threshold.  

**Penalties:**  
- Minimum **12-month driving disqualification** (3 years if second offence within 10 years).  
- Unlimited fine.  
- Up to **6 months imprisonment**.  
- Criminal record.  

**Defences:**  
- **Prescription defence** – if the drug was prescribed and taken as directed, you may avoid liability. You’d need medical evidence.  
- **Procedural issues** – if the blood sample was taken, stored, or tested incorrectly.  
- **Not driving / not in charge** – if police can’t prove you were in control of the vehicle.  

---

## 🔹 2. Section 4 – “Unfit to Drive Through Drugs”  
**The law:**  
- It’s an offence to drive (or attempt to drive) while unfit due to drugs.  
- The prosecution must prove **impairment**, usually through:  
  - Officer’s observations (e.g. eyes glazed, slurred speech, poor coordination).  
  - Evidence from “field impairment tests.”  
  - Medical practitioner’s opinion.  
  - Driving behaviour (swerving, erratic speed, collision).  

**Penalties:**  
- Same as Section 5A:  
  - Minimum **12-month driving disqualification** (3 years if repeat offence).  
  - Unlimited fine.  
  - Up to **6 months imprisonment**.  

**Defences:**  
- **Not impaired** – you may accept you had drugs in your system but argue you were not unfit to drive (harder if officer’s notes or video show obvious impairment).  
- **Alternative cause** – fatigue, illness, or another factor explained your behaviour, not drugs.  
- **Procedural issues** – mistakes in assessment or failure to follow correct procedure.  

---

## 🔹 Key Differences  
- **Section 5A** is essentially *strict liability* → over the limit = guilty, unless you have a prescription defence or testing was flawed.  
- **Section 4** is more subjective → prosecution must prove actual impairment, not just presence of drugs.  
- Police often charge both offences “in the alternative” if evidence allows, leaving the court to decide.  

---

⚖️ **Summary**:  
- **Over the limit (s.5A):** hard to defend, but clear-cut.  
- **Unfit (s.4):** more open to challenge, but riskier if evidence of impairment is strong.

   - Rare for drug driving — because it carries a mandatory ban if convicted.

  - If guilty → sentence can be passed same day (often is).  
  - If not guilty → case listed for trial.

- **Fine**: usually means-tested.  
- **Prison**: only in more serious cases (e.g. crash, injury, high impairment).  
- **Criminal record**: the conviction will appear on DBS checks.  
- **Insurance impact**: premiums rise significantly, sometimes refusal of cover.

## 🔹 6. If Not Guilty  
- Case goes to trial in the Magistrates’ Court.  
- Prosecution evidence: blood test, police observations, expert testimony if needed.  
- Defence evidence: cross-examining police, medical records, prescription details, expert reports.  
- Magistrates decide verdict.  

---

⚖️ **Key point**:  
The voluntary interview is usually the *last step in the investigation*. After that, it’s about whether CPS think there’s enough evidence to charge you.

# 💊 Prescription Defence in Drug Driving Cases  

## 🔹 1. Legal Basis  
- Applies to **Section 5A (over the limit)** offences.  
- If you were taking a **controlled drug lawfully** under prescription, **and according to medical guidance**, you may not be guilty even if blood levels exceed the legal limit.  
- The defence is essentially:  
  > “I took this drug exactly as prescribed, and it did not impair my driving.”  

---

## 🔹 2. What Must Be Proven  
Courts typically look for evidence that:  

1. **Prescription was legitimate**  
   - Written by a licensed doctor or medical practitioner.  
   - Correctly dispensed by a pharmacy.  

2. **Taken exactly as directed**  
   - Dosage, timing, frequency all followed.  
   - No extra doses or mixing with other substances not advised.  

3. **Knowledge of effects**  
   - You were informed of possible side effects (drowsiness, impaired coordination).  
   - You did not ignore warnings.  

4. **No impairment**  
   - Even if above legal limit, you were not unfit to drive (Section 4 still matters).  

---

## 🔹 3. Evidence That Strengthens the Defence  
- **Doctor’s letter** confirming prescription and dosage.  
- **Pharmacy labels or records** showing medication was dispensed correctly.  
- **Medical notes** regarding side effects or monitoring.  
- **Driving history or witness testimony** showing you were alert and competent.  
- **Blood levels** interpreted in context — some medications can push levels above legal limits even when taken correctly.  

---

## 🔹 4. Court Approach  
- Courts weigh whether **over-the-limit reading reflects unlawful or impaired use**.  
- If you can show proper prescription and adherence, the court may **accept it as a lawful defence**, potentially avoiding conviction.  
- Note: **admission of driving while impaired by non-prescribed drugs cannot use this defence**.  

---

## 🔹 5. Practical Implications for the Interview  
- Bring proof of prescription to your solicitor **before** the interview.  
- Solicitor can advise:  
  - Give a **prepared statement** outlining prescription use, or  
  - Say **no comment** on questions that could incriminate if proof isn’t ready.  

---

⚖️ **Bottom line:**  
A prescription defence can completely neutralise a Section 5A charge **if you have proper documentation and evidence of lawful use**. But without proof, talking to police freely can weaken your case.
