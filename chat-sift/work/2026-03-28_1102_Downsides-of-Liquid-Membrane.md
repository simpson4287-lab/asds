[M1] ME (2026-03-28 11:02):
Are there any downsides to using liquid membrane in sub base 

[M2] AI (2026-03-28 11:02):
Yes. It can work, but there are a few downsides if you’re thinking of painting a **liquid membrane onto a sub-base**.

The main issue is that a sub-base is usually **not a clean, stable, ideal waterproofing surface**. It is often rough, dusty, porous, and can move a bit. Liquid membrane tends to work best on a **sound, well-prepared substrate** like concrete, screed, cement board, or masonry, not loose or semi-open sub-base material.

Main drawbacks:

- **Poor adhesion risk**  
  If the sub-base is dusty, granular, or not well bound, the membrane may not key properly and can peel or form weak patches.

- **Damage during construction**  
  Even if it cures fine, once you walk on it, drag materials over it, or put layers on top, it can get punctured or scuffed quite easily unless protected.

- **It can trap moisture in the wrong place**  
  If water is already coming up through the ground or moving through the build-up, a membrane in the wrong layer can hold moisture where you do not want it, rather than letting it drain or vent.

- **Not a replacement for drainage**  
  It does not solve the real issue if the area needs fall, ventilation gap, gravel drainage, or a proper DPM/DPC arrangement. It only creates a barrier.

- **Uneven coverage**  
  On rough sub-base surfaces it is harder to guarantee the correct thickness everywhere, so weak spots are common.

- **Long-term durability uncertainty**  
  In hidden external areas, especially where dampness is persistent, freeze-thaw, movement, and dirt contamination can reduce confidence compared with a proper sheet membrane or physical DPM detail.

For your kind of external cabin/gap build-up, liquid membrane is usually best seen as a **secondary belt-and-braces detail**, not the primary waterproofing strategy. It is more useful on a **solid concrete edge, slab, plinth, or upstand** than directly on general sub-base.

A better hierarchy is usually:

1. **Drainage and fall first**
2. **Physical separation layer / DPM where needed**
3. **Proper upstands and overlaps**
4. **Ventilation gap maintained**
5. Liquid membrane only as a local sealing aid, not the whole answer

So the downside is not that it is “bad,” but that it is easy to use it in a place where it gives a false sense of security.

For your setup, using it selectively on a **clean concrete surface or sole plate interface** makes much more sense than relying on it over the sub-base itself.

On **concrete**, a liquid membrane can be useful, but there are still some downsides:

  Too thin and it is weak. Too thick and curing can be poor. Corners and edges are the common failure points.

  A lot of “liquid membrane” products are really intended for bathrooms, balconies under finishes, or protected detailing, not open outdoor exposure.

- **It can hold water above it if the falls are poor**  
  So it helps with separation from the concrete, but it does not replace drainage or slope.

[M8] AI (2026-03-28 11:10):
Usually **no** — not if the aim is to avoid trapping water.

If you add **another sheet membrane and turn it up at the edges**, you can easily create a **tray effect**. That can be good in some assemblies, but in your case it sounds like the risk is the opposite: any water that gets in from above, sides, or condensation then has **nowhere easy to go**.

For your kind of detail, the safer principle is generally:

- **isolate the timber from the concrete**
- **allow incidental water to escape**
- **avoid making a sealed bath unless the whole system is designed that way**

So rather than a full turned-up sheet, what usually makes more sense is:

- **local DPC/DPM strips or pads** under bearers/pack points
- maybe a **bonded liquid membrane on the concrete** as a clean working surface
- keep small routes for drainage/drying rather than sealing the perimeter

A turned-up sheet is more appropriate when:
- the whole base is intentionally being detailed as a waterproof tray
- there is a controlled outlet/drain path
- the upper layers are also designed to stop water ingress

Without that, it can trap:
- rain blowing in
- runoff from adjacent faces
- condensation
- construction moisture

So in your case, I would be cautious about a full perimeter-turned sheet.

Better options are:

**Option A — open-drying approach**
- liquid membrane on concrete
- plastic packers
- DPC strip under timber contact
- edges left able to dry/vent

**Option B — local splash control**
- DPC only where timber bears
- no full tray
- edge details shaped so water sheds away rather than being retained

The key question is: do you want to **exclude all water**, or just make sure that if a little gets in, it **cannot sit there**?  
For an external detail, the second approach is often more forgiving.

So yes, a turned-up sheet **could** prevent wetting from below, but it also **could seal water in**, and that is the main downside. In your setup I would lean toward **local isolation, not full containment**.

You **can** jet wash it first, but I would be careful about the **“abrasive the top”** part.

Best aim is:
**clean the concrete well without weakening the surface**.

Good:
- remove dirt
- remove algae
- remove dust
- remove loose laitance or flaky bits
- flush out debris from pits/cracks

Less good:
- blasting so hard that you **start scouring soft surface concrete**
- creating a rough, friable top layer
- forcing lots of water deep into the slab right before coating

So I would **jet wash for cleaning**, not for aggressive mechanical preparation.

What I’d do:
1. Sweep first
2. Jet wash at a sensible distance
3. Let it dry properly
4. Check whether the surface feels **sound and hard**, not chalky or sandy
5. Then coat

If the surface is glossy, contaminated, or has weak laitance, a **light mechanical key** can help — but that is usually better done with a **stiff brush, abrasion, or light grinding**, not trying to use water pressure as a substitute.

The big issue is drying:
with a bitumen DPM, you do **not** want the concrete surface still wet when you apply it. Slight residual slab moisture deep down is one thing; a visibly damp or freshly saturated surface is another.

So:
- **Yes** to jet washing for cleaning
- **No** to trying to erode the surface on purpose
- **Yes** to waiting for it to dry thoroughly

A simple rule:
if, after drying, you rub the surface with your hand and get **dust/chalk/grit**, it needs more prep before coating.

For your use, the ideal surface is:
**clean, hard, dust-free, dry at the surface, and not polished smooth**.
