[M2] AI (2026-06-12 15:15):
That forum post describes a **very plausible cause**, especially because your fault appears at a repeatable road speed and then remains latched until the ignition is cycled.

On the 2007 Saab 9-5, I would put the likely causes in this order:

1. **Cracked or heavily corroded front ABS reluctor ring**
2. Wheel-speed sensor or its wiring
3. Excessive sensor air gap, wheel-bearing play or driveshaft movement
4. ABS control module fault

The reluctor ring is the toothed ring on the outer CV joint/driveshaft. Rust can expand underneath it and split it. The crack produces one abnormally large “tooth gap.” At lower speeds the ABS controller may tolerate or misinterpret it, but around a particular speed the signal becomes implausible compared with the other wheels, so it records a fault and disables ABS/traction control for the rest of that ignition cycle. Saab owners report this exact speed-dependent behaviour from cracked front rings. citeturn240430search3turn240430search5turn240430search17

### What I would do first

Have the car scanned with **Tech 2 or another scanner that can read Saab ABS codes and live wheel-speed data**. A basic engine-code reader normally will not access the ABS module.

During a controlled road test, monitor all four wheel speeds. Near 60 mph, one may:

- momentarily drop,
- jump,
- fluctuate,
- or differ from the other three.

That identifies the corner before parts are replaced. A stored code might say something such as front-left wheel-speed signal, but it does not automatically prove the sensor itself is defective—the sensor may simply be reporting a damaged ring.

Then visually inspect both front reluctor rings by slowly rotating each wheel/driveshaft. Look closely for:

- a hairline split between two teeth;
- rust lifting or enlarging the ring;
- missing or damaged teeth;
- a ring that is no longer concentric;
- metallic debris on the sensor;
- damaged wiring near the strut/hub;
- excessive wheel-bearing play.

A damaged tone ring, excessive hub movement, sensor air-gap problems and intermittent wiring can all create wheel-speed dropouts. citeturn240430search8turn240430search12

### Ring versus complete driveshaft

The ring itself is inexpensive, but replacing it generally requires removing the driveshaft from the hub and cleaning the CV-joint surface thoroughly. Depending on corrosion, labour rates and the condition of the CV joint, fitting a complete rebuilt or good-quality driveshaft can be economically sensible. I would not replace the complete shaft automatically, though—a competent garage can assess whether the existing joint and splines are worth retaining.

### Important distinction

When you say it “turns on its ABS and brakes,” do you mean:

- the **ABS and red brake warning lights illuminate**, or
- the car physically applies ABS, with pedal vibration or braking intervention?

If it is only the warning lamps, conventional hydraulic braking should remain, but ABS and traction/stability functions may be unavailable. If it actually brakes or pulses unexpectedly at motorway speed, treat it as urgent and avoid driving it except for diagnosis, because a corrupt wheel-speed signal can cause inappropriate intervention before the system finally disables itself.

Based on the repeatable **approximately 60 mph threshold**, I would investigate the front reluctor rings and wheel-speed live data before considering an ABS module.

[M6] AI (2026-06-14 18:05):
Yes, but the **9-5 suits subtle bodywork much better than aggressive kits**. The Aero HOT already has the right shape: low, long, understated. The best look is usually “factory-plus,” not Fast & Furious.

For a 2007 Saab 9-5 Aero, I’d consider:

**1. Factory Aero / Anniversary-style trim**
This is the safest route. Aero bumpers, side skirts, boot lip spoiler, and the right wheels make it look purposeful without ruining the car. If yours is already Aero, it may already have most of this.

**2. Hirsch-style look**
Hirsch upgrades were the tasteful Saab performance route: slightly sharper wheels, lower stance, discreet badging, subtle exhaust. Proper Hirsch parts are rare and expensive, but the *style* suits the car very well.

**3. Lowering springs**
A mild drop, around **25–30 mm**, probably transforms the look more than a body kit. Too low looks wrong and hurts ride quality. Eibach/Hirsch/Bilstein-type setups are the kind of direction I’d look at.

**4. Front lip / splitter**
A subtle black or body-coloured front lip can work. Avoid big universal splitters with rods; they usually look out of place on a 9-5.

**5. Boot lip spoiler**
Small lip spoiler: yes. Big wing: no. The 9-5 rear end looks best with a restrained spoiler.

**6. Wheels**
This is probably the biggest visual upgrade. Saab 17" or 18" wheels suit it well. Anything too modern, blacked-out, or oversized can make the car look cheaper rather than sportier.

I’d avoid wide arches, huge side skirts, aftermarket vented bonnets, universal diffusers, big wings, and cheap fibreglass bumper kits. The 9-5 does not really have the stance/proportions for a loud body kit. It looks better as a stealthy executive turbo car.

Best recipe, in my opinion: **Aero/Hirsch look, 18" wheels, mild lowering, clean paint, subtle exhaust tip, maybe a small front lip and boot lip.** That keeps the Saab character but gives it a sharper road presence.
