## 2. Why separation is strategically important

Keeping the detailed clinical material separate achieves several things:

### a) It avoids medical overreach in the legal narrative
Courts and prosecutors are wary of defence documents that:
- appear to “diagnose from the dock”, or
- draw clinical conclusions without expert framing.

Your current document avoids this entirely by:
- describing *effects and vulnerability*, not diagnoses,
- attributing diagnoses to treating clinicians,
- and stopping short of asserting medical causation.

A separate clinical file preserves that discipline.

---

### b) It preserves disclosure control
Your clinical notes likely include:
- sensitive historical material,
- information irrelevant to the incident,
- matters that could be misunderstood or selectively quoted.

By keeping them separate:
- your solicitor can **decide what to disclose, when, and why**,
- extracts can be used surgically (e.g. confirmation of diagnosis, medication instability),
- without opening the entire clinical history to scrutiny.

---

### c) It aligns with how CPS and courts actually work
In practice:

- **Legal narrative** → reviewed by CPS lawyer  
- **Clinical records** → reviewed only if relevance is established  
- **Expert evidence** → introduced deliberately, not by accident  

Your current pack sits exactly where it should in that pipeline.

## 3. How the two documents should interact (best practice)

The optimal structure is:

### 1. Master consolidated legal / contextual reference  
(What you already have)

- Mentions diagnoses **factually** (“diagnosed ADHD”, “treatment-resistant MDD”)
- Describes **functional impact** and **vulnerability**
- States that detailed clinical records exist
- Does **not** annex raw clinical notes

### 2. Separate clinical dossier (held by solicitor)
Containing:
- psychiatrist letters,
- GP summaries,
- referral confirmations,
- medication change records,
- any formal diagnostic formulations.

Used:
- reactively (if CPS queries clinical assertions),
- or proactively (if mitigation or diversion is pursued).

### 3. Optional: short **clinical summary letter**
If needed later, this is often more powerful than raw notes:
- a brief letter from a treating clinician,
- confirming diagnoses, medication instability, and functional impact,
- without disclosing everything else.
