Yes — **alcoholism is generally treated as a disease too**, though the more current medical term is **alcohol use disorder** or **alcohol dependence**, not “alcoholism”.

The logic is very similar to obesity:

**Obesity** can involve behaviour, environment, biology, genetics, reward systems, hormones, mental health, trauma, medication effects, social factors, and personal choices. It is still classed by major medical bodies as a chronic complex condition/disease because it can impair health and is not simply a moral failing. WHO’s ICD-11 describes obesity as a chronic complex condition involving excessive adiposity that can impair health. citeturn155031search7

**Alcohol dependence / alcohol use disorder** is also medically classified. ICD-11 includes alcohol-related diagnoses such as alcohol dependence, harmful pattern of alcohol use, alcohol withdrawal and alcohol-induced mental disorders. citeturn155031search9 The NHS also treats alcohol-use disorder as a diagnosable condition with assessment, risks, and medications such as acamprosate, naltrexone and disulfiram. citeturn155031search3

The important bit: calling alcoholism a disease **doesn’t mean the person has no agency at all**. It means the condition involves altered control, craving, reinforcement, withdrawal, tolerance, relapse risk, and physical/psychological harm. So responsibility shifts from **“you’re bad”** to **“you have a condition that needs management, treatment, support, and protective choices.”**

A fair comparison would be:

> **Obesity is not just “eating too much”; alcoholism is not just “drinking too much.”**

Both can involve behaviour, but both can also involve biological vulnerability, learned coping, reward circuitry, environment, shame, stress, and relapse loops.

So yes: **if obesity can be recognised as a disease, alcoholism absolutely can be too**. In fact, alcoholism/alcohol dependence has arguably had a disease model in medicine for longer than obesity has had broad acceptance as one.
