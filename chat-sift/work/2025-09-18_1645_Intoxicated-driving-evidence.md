For an offence like **drink driving**, the prosecution generally needs to prove two things:  

1. **You were driving (or in charge of) the vehicle**  
2. **You were over the legal limit (or unfit through drink/drugs) at the time of driving**  

---

### The role of camera footage  
- CCTV or dashcam evidence showing you appearing *intoxicated* before getting into the car might raise suspicion, but **“appearing intoxicated” isn’t proof**.  
- Behaviour on video can support the case, but it isn’t enough on its own to establish the legal alcohol/drug level.  

---

### The roadside or station test  
- Normally, police use a roadside breath test (screening) and then require an **evidential breath, blood, or urine test** at the police station.  
- The evidential test result is what counts in court.  

---

### Timing issue (delay between driving and test)  
- If there’s a delay, the defence can argue that your alcohol level at the time of testing might not reflect your level **at the time of driving**.  
- The prosecution may call an expert (a forensic toxicologist) to do a **“back calculation”** based on how the body absorbs and eliminates alcohol, estimating what your level would have been when driving.  
- But this isn’t always straightforward, and the longer the delay, the harder it is to prove beyond reasonable doubt.  

---

### Key point  
Camera footage alone wouldn’t normally be *sufficient evidence*. The prosecution would usually need:  
- An evidential test result, and  
- A reliable way to link that result back to when you were driving.

### 1. What the “hip flask defence” is  
- A driver claims they only drank **after driving but before being tested** (e.g. at home, in a pub, or even while waiting).

---

### 2. The prosecution’s burden  
The Crown must prove **beyond reasonable doubt** that you were:  
- Over the limit *at the time of driving*, not just later.  
- If there’s a significant delay, they often need expert toxicological evidence to “back calculate” your likely alcohol level when driving.  

---

### 3. How back-calculation works  
A forensic toxicologist will look at:  
- Your test result (e.g. breath/blood alcohol concentration).  
- The time between driving and the test.  
- Average rates of alcohol absorption and elimination.  
They then estimate whether you were likely above the limit at the time you drove.  

But —  
- Alcohol absorbs at different rates depending on food, body type, sex, etc.  
- These assumptions can be challenged by the defence.  

---

### 4. What the defence can argue  
- **Hip flask defence**: “I only drank after I finished driving.”  
- **Unreliable back-calculation**: human metabolism is too variable for certainty.  
- **Unexplained delay**: if police didn’t act promptly, doubt arises over what your alcohol level was while driving.  

---

### 5. What the court considers  
- CCTV, witness statements, or officer observations (slurred speech, unsteady gait, smell of alcohol).  
- Whether your account of *when* you drank is consistent and believable.  
- Expert evidence from both prosecution and defence (toxicologists).  

---

✅ **Bottom line:** If there’s a long gap between alleged driving and the evidential test, the prosecution usually needs strong forensic evidence plus credibility findings to secure a conviction. - If the test is delayed (e.g. due to accident, medical issues, or late discovery of the driver), the police know the defence might argue alcohol was consumed after driving.

  - They might gather CCTV or witness evidence to establish you hadn’t been drinking after driving.

- If police acted quickly and by the book, the prosecution case is stronger.  
- If there were **avoidable delays**, a defence solicitor can argue the result isn’t reliable, or that there’s a real possibility of post-driving drinking.  

---

✅ **Summary**: Police try to minimise delay because it weakens their case. If there *is* a delay, they use blood/urine testing and careful questioning to shut down a possible hip flask defence.

That’s a sharp follow-up 👍 — **drug-driving cases** are similar in principle to drink-driving, but there are some important differences.  

---

## 🚗 1. The legal tests  
- **Drink-driving**: prosecution must prove you were **over the alcohol limit while driving**.  
- **Drug-driving** (s.5A Road Traffic Act 1988): prosecution must prove you were **over a set blood concentration limit** for a controlled drug *while driving*.  
   - The limits are very low and designed so that even trace levels can lead to prosecution (e.g. cannabis, cocaine).  
   - For some drugs (like diazepam or morphine), the limits are set to avoid penalising normal therapeutic use.  

---

## 🧪 2. Testing procedure  
- Police can use a **roadside saliva test** (swab) to detect cannabis or cocaine.  
- If positive, you’ll be arrested and required to give a **blood sample at the station or hospital**.  
- Unlike alcohol, there’s no evidential breath test — **blood is the main evidence**.  

---

## ⏱ 3. Timing and delay  
- With alcohol, a delay weakens the case because your level can change rapidly (and “hip flask” drinking can confuse the picture).  
- With drugs, it’s different:  
  - Most drugs **stay detectable in blood for hours or even days**, so a delay of 1–4 hours is less damaging to the prosecution.  
  - There’s no real “hip flask” equivalent — you can’t credibly claim you just took a line of cocaine *after* you parked the car unless you have very strong proof.  

---

## ⚖️ 4. Back-calculation  
- Alcohol: toxicologists often try to back-calculate your level at the time of driving.  
- Drugs: back-calculation is **rare**. Instead, the law is simpler — if your blood sample shows you were over the specified limit, that’s usually enough.  
- Timing only becomes an issue if you argue you took the drug **after driving**, but courts are sceptical unless you can show solid evidence.  

---

## 👩‍⚖️ 5. Court considerations  
- **Alcohol cases** often hinge on timing, absorption, and metabolism rates.  
- **Drug cases** usually hinge on:  
  - Was the sample taken properly?  
  - Did the lab analysis follow procedure?  
  - Can you prove the drug entered your system *after* you stopped driving? (very rare to succeed).  

---

## ✅ Summary: Alcohol vs. Drugs  
- **Alcohol**: timing of the test is critical — long delays can create reasonable doubt (hip flask defence).  
- **Drugs**: timing matters much less — presence above the limit in blood is usually enough, even with hours of delay.

  - The drug’s impairing effects may have worn off by the time of the test.
