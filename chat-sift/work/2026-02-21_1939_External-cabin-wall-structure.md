## The main technical issues in the sketch (the bits I would change)
### 1) You’re missing a proper rainscreen / drainage & ventilation cavity
Right now the outer face looks like **painted OSB** acting as the weathering layer. That’s the weak point.

**Better:**  
Outside → inside should usually include:
- **Cladding / rainscreen** (timber, fibre-cement, composite, etc.)
- **Ventilated cavity** (typically 20–25 mm) on vertical battens
- **Breather membrane (WRB)** behind the cavity
- **Sheathing** (OSB/ply/fibreboard) *or* racking method
- **Studs + insulation**
- Existing cabin wall

Reason: any water that gets past cladding must be able to **drain and dry**. Painted OSB as the primary weather skin tends to age poorly at joints/fixings and is unforgiving.

### 2) The breather membrane position looks wrong/ambiguous
Your label “Semi-Breath Membrane Sheet” appears to be **between the OSB and the studs/insulation**.

In most robust details, the **breather membrane goes on the cold side, outside of the sheathing**, immediately behind the ventilated cavity/cladding. Putting it between sheathing and studs is unusual and can make the sheathing sit on the “wrong” side of the drying path.

### 3) Moisture risk: don’t create a vapour trap against the cabin wall
With a log/timber cabin, you really want the assembly to be able to **dry outward**.

Watch-outs:
- If you put **OSB + paint** outside (vapour-resistant), *and* you seal the inside interface heavily, you can trap moisture in the timber.
- Prefer **vapour-open outer layers** (breather membrane + ventilated cavity + rainscreen).

### 4) Interface sealing: aim for “wind-tight”, not “waterproof”
Your tape/foam idea is good, but the goal at the cabin wall is:
- stop drafts / wind washing through the wool
- allow incidental moisture to migrate out

So: **compressible sealing + sensible laps**, but avoid turning that junction into a fully sealed moisture dam.
