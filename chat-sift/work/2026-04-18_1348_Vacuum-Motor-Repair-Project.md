[M2] AI (2026-04-18 13:48):
Yes — **very possibly repairable**, and for learning purposes it is actually a decent candidate.

From your photo and symptom sequence, the most likely story is:

1. it was heavily clogged,
2. airflow was poor,
3. after unclogging, something in the **motor / brushroll / belt area** was already damaged or suddenly free enough to load differently,
4. then it **progressively struggled, overheated, and smoked**.

Because the smoke/smell came from the lower front area you’re pointing at, that does **not automatically mean the motor itself is dead**. It could be:

- **belt slipping/burning**
- **brush roller jammed or tight bearings**
- **debris still wrapped around the brush spindle**
- **motor fan chamber packed with dust**
- **motor carbon brushes badly worn**
- **motor commutator arcing**
- **thermal protection cycling before final overheating**

So: **can the motor be replaced?**  
Usually yes, in principle. On vacuums like this the motor is often a replaceable module, or at least removable. But whether it is worth doing economically is a separate question.

For your stated goal — **practising fault-finding and repair in the cabin** — I’d say **absolutely yes, it’s a good project**, with one caveat:

**treat it as a mains appliance rebuild, not a casual poke-around job.**

What I’d do:

### Initial verdict
Do **not** power it again yet.

If it has already produced smoke, another run could turn a belt problem into a cooked motor, or a weak motor into a fully burnt one.

### Best diagnostic order
With it unplugged:

- remove the base/brush cover
- inspect the **belt**
- turn the **brush roller by hand**
- check for hair/thread/string wound around the ends
- inspect for melted plastic near the belt path
- smell the lower housing:
  - **rubber/burnt tyre smell** = belt likely
  - **sharp electrical/varnish smell** = motor windings/brushes more likely

Then, if accessible:

- inspect the motor chamber for compacted dust
- look for black soot around brush gear / commutator vents
- check whether the motor shaft spins freely

### Signs it is still a good restoration candidate
Good candidate if:

- housing is intact
- switches/cable look sound
- brushroll parts are present
- motor spins but smells rough / brushes worn
- only the belt or brushroll is seized

Poor candidate if:

- winding smell is very strong and persistent
- commutator is badly burnt
- fan housing melted
- armature has obvious rub damage
- electrical insulation looks cooked

### Realistically, what may have failed
Most likely order, from easiest to nastiest:

1. **belt burned**
2. **brush bar seized**
3. **motor choked with dust and overheated**
4. **carbon brushes worn or stuck**
5. **motor armature/windings damaged**

### For a learning project
This is actually useful because it teaches:

- airflow dependency in vacuums
- series motor behaviour under blocked cooling
- mechanical drag diagnosis
- belt drive failures
- brush/commutator inspection
- teardown and reassembly discipline

That makes it a very suitable “man cabin electrical fix” item.

### Sensible approach
Think of it in 3 levels:

**Level 1: recoverable without motor replacement**
- clean all ducts
- free brushroll
- replace belt
- clean motor cooling path

**Level 2: serviceable motor**
- replace brushes
- clean commutator carefully
- replace thermal fuse only if you have proved the root cause and are competent

**Level 3: full donor/parts swap**
- replace motor module from donor machine / spare part

### Important caution
Because it is a **mains appliance**, I would avoid casual “just wire a new motor in” experimentation unless:

- insulation is sound
- strain relief is intact
- earth/double-insulation arrangement is preserved exactly
- no thermal cut-out or safety device is bypassed

That is the line between a satisfying repair and an unsafe one.
