### **3.1 Diagnostic Algorithms of the ADOS-2 (pp. 131-133)**  
Each module of the ADOS-2 uses a specific algorithm to turn raw observation codes into diagnostic results. These algorithms consider the individual’s **age**, **language level**, and **observed social and communicative behavior**.  

- **Module 1:**  
  Two algorithms exist depending on language use.  
  - *Few or no words*: for children producing fewer than five meaningful words.  
  - *Some words*: for those who produce five or more words.  
- **Module 2:**  
  Divided by **age** rather than vocabulary.  
  - *Under 5 years*: for younger children.  
  - *5 years or older*: for older children using phrase speech.  
- **Modules 3 and 4:**  
  Each has a single algorithm for **verbally fluent** individuals—Module 3 for children/adolescents, Module 4 for adolescents/adults.  
  The Module 4 algorithm is identical to that in the original ADOS.
- **Toddler Module (T):**  
  Introduced later; has two algorithms: one for *younger children or those with few/no words* and another for *older toddlers with some words*.  

The revision of algorithms in the ADOS-2 aimed to minimize the effect of **language ability** on autism scoring. Still, verbal aptitude remains a crucial adaptive factor, so the chosen module (and item A1: “Overall level of non-echoed spoken language”) serves as a proxy for language development.  

The **TEA (Trastorno del Espectro Autista)** category includes individuals previously classified as *autism*, *atypical autism*, *PDD-NOS*, or *Asperger syndrome*.

### **3.2 ADOS-2 Classifications (p. 133-134)**  
From the algorithm scores, the examiner sums subscale scores—Communication (C), Reciprocal Social Interaction (ISR), and sometimes Restricted/Repetitive Behavior (RRB)—and compares them with cutoff points.  
Three outcome categories are possible:
1. **Autism**
2. **Autism Spectrum (TEA no autista)**
3. **Non-spectrum**

Cutoffs vary per module. These categories are **behavioral descriptions**, not stand-alone clinical diagnoses; they must be integrated with parent reports, cognitive testing, developmental history, and professional judgment.

---

### **3.3 Comparison Score (p. 134)**  
To make scores comparable across ages and modules, the ADOS-2 introduces a **Comparison Score (Puntuación Comparativa)**.  
- It ranges from **1 to 10**, with higher scores indicating more severe autism-related behaviors.  
- It is derived from the total algorithm score and calibrated using age and language level.  
- The Comparison Score allows progress tracking over time or between modules without re-interpreting cutoffs each time.

### **3.4 Scoring the ADOS-2 (pp. 135-155)**  

#### **Manual Scoring**
- After completing the observation, raw item codes (0–3) are transferred to the **algorithm form**.  
- The examiner sums Communication and Social-Interaction items (and RRBs if required).  
- The totals are compared with the module’s cutoffs to determine classification.  
- The process requires careful reading of item-by-item coding guidelines to avoid bias.  
- A **Summary Form** records the participant’s demographic data, selected module, and total scores for communication, social interaction, restricted/repetitive behaviors, and overall total.

#### **Computerized Scoring**
- The **ADOS-2 Scoring Software** automates entry, summation, and classification.
- Data from multiple sessions can be compiled to show developmental changes.
- The system performs error checking, ensures algorithms are correctly applied, and outputs standardized comparison scores and graphs.
- Both manual and computerized methods yield the same classifications if data are entered correctly.

### **3.5 Interpretation of ADOS-2 Results (pp. 156-178)**  

#### **Validity and Interpretation**
- The ADOS-2 is an *observation-based* diagnostic tool, not a full diagnostic system.  
- It must be interpreted alongside background data—interviews, developmental history, and other assessments.  
- A “positive” ADOS-2 result (Autism/TEA) indicates that observed behaviors are consistent with the autism spectrum, but does not alone confirm the disorder.

#### **Interpreting Classifications**
- **Autism classification:** clear, frequent deficits in reciprocal social interaction and communication, plus repetitive behaviors.  
- **Autism Spectrum classification:** similar difficulties but fewer or less severe; often includes those previously labeled Asperger’s or atypical autism.  
- **Non-spectrum classification:** behaviors inconsistent with TEA during the observation, though other disorders may still be present.  

Scores should not be compared directly across modules unless converted to Comparison Scores. The classification reflects *observed* behavior, not necessarily the child’s full ability range.

#### **Interpreting the Comparison Score**
- The Comparison Score quantifies the *severity* of autistic symptoms relative to other individuals with TEA, standardizing across age/language differences.
- For instance:  
  - Scores 1–3 = minimal/no autism-related traits  
  - Scores 4–5 = mild-moderate levels  
  - Scores 6–10 = high or severe levels
- This facilitates longitudinal tracking and outcome studies.

#### **Other Methods of Comparison Over Time (pp. 162-178)**
- In addition to the Comparison Score, evaluators can use repeated ADOS-2 administrations to detect **behavioral change**, though direct comparison of raw scores is discouraged.
- The chapter discusses **test-retest reliability**, inter-rater agreement, and regression analyses showing that **verbal IQ** and **maternal education** have modest predictive effects on scores, while **chronological age** and **nonverbal IQ** do not significantly bias results.
- Emphasis is placed on **standardization**, **examiner training**, and **multi-source clinical judgment** for reliable interpretation.
