---

### ⚖️ 1. Definition of “Road” or “Public Place”
Most driving offences — such as *“driving while unfit through drugs”* or *“in charge while unfit”* — require that the driving took place **on a road or other public place**.

- **“Road”** is any highway or area to which the public has access.  
- **“Public place”** includes car parks, forecourts, and private land *if* the public *can or does* access it (even informally).  
- However, **if the area was closed**, **fenced**, or **clearly marked as out of bounds**, then **it may *not* count as a public place** for the purposes of the offence.

So if you were in a **park after hours, a locked car park, or a gated area with restricted access**, your solicitor might argue:
> “At the time, the area was not accessible to the general public and therefore does not meet the legal definition of a ‘public place’.”

---

### 🧭 2. Supporting Factors That Strengthen That Argument
To strengthen that defence, evidence should show:
- Gates were locked or signs said *“Closed”* / *“No Public Access”*.  
- There was no active right of way (e.g., public footpath).  
- You had a **lawful reason** to be there (e.g., sheltering, confused, unwell) rather than being there for leisure or driving purposes.  
- CCTV or police bodycam shows **no other members of the public present**.  
- The officer’s notes or witness statements confirm that **the area was closed**.

---

### ⚖️ 3. How It Can Affect the Outcome
If the defence successfully argues that the location was *not* a “road or public place”:
- **The offence may not be made out in law.**
- Even if you were in the car, **the “in charge” element may fail** if the public safety risk was minimal (e.g., car stationary, secluded area, no risk to others).

---

### 🧠 4. Important Caveat
Courts interpret “public place” **very broadly** — even a privately owned area can qualify *if* the public are *allowed or accustomed to go there*, even without explicit permission.  
So the key question is **“Was the public able to access it at that moment in time?”**, not just *who owned it*.

- A *“public place”* means anywhere the public **can and do** go, *with permission or by habit*, even if privately owned (like a supermarket car park).

### ⚖️ 1.  General Legal Framework

Under UK law (mainly the **Occupiers’ Liability Acts 1957 & 1984** and common-law negligence principles), a *duty of care* arises when:

1. The defendant **has control over the premises** (e.g. the council or park operator);  
2. It is **reasonably foreseeable** that someone might suffer harm; and  
3. It is **fair, just and reasonable** to impose a duty in the circumstances.

So yes, **a duty can exist** — but it’s limited and depends on what the occupier actually knew or should have done.

---

### 🏞️ 2.  When a Duty of Care *Might* Exist in Your Scenario

If park staff or the council **became aware** (or reasonably should have been aware) that:
- A vehicle was still present at closing time, **and**
- That presence suggested someone might be **lost, vulnerable, in distress, or unwell**,  

then there’s an arguable duty to **make at least minimal enquiries** before locking or leaving the site — e.g.:

- Checking the area or vehicle visually;  
- Leaving the main gate open temporarily while contact is attempted;  
- Notifying police or security if there’s genuine concern.

Failing to do so could amount to a **breach of duty**, particularly if later harm occurred (e.g., the person was injured, collapsed, or required emergency help).

---

### 🧭 3.  When a Duty is *Less Likely* to Exist

- If the car’s presence was not noticed;  
- If staff reasonably believed everyone had left;  
- If no signs of distress or emergency were apparent;  
then a court would probably say no practical duty arose.  
Authorities are **not expected to guarantee safety**, only to take *reasonable* care based on what’s foreseeable.

---

### ⚖️ 4.  The Type of Duty

If a duty existed, it would likely fall under **the Occupiers’ Liability Act 1984**, which covers **non-visitors** (people on land without permission, such as after hours).  
That Act still imposes a duty *if the occupier knows of the danger and knows someone may be near it* — essentially, “don’t ignore a known risk of serious harm.”

So a large remote park closing for the night **doesn’t automatically owe a duty to search every corner**, but if they know a lone car remains, they may owe a **limited humanitarian duty** to check welfare or alert authorities.

You could reasonably argue:

That would be a **moral and potentially legal** expectation — especially if the park is council-run and staff are trained to look out for public welfare.
