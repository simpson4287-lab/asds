People previously diagnosed with Asperger’s now fall under **Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), typically Level 1** — meaning they have average-to-high cognitive and language abilities but still experience social, sensory, or behavioural challenges.

Under DSM-5, **ASD is described using support levels rather than separate labels:**

---

## 🧩 **ASD Support Levels**

| ASD Level | Core Description | Social Interaction | Behaviour/Rigidity | Support Needs |
|----------|------------------|--------------------|--------------------|---------------|
| **Level 1 – Requiring Support** *(where most people historically labelled Asperger’s sit)* | Can communicate and function independently, but autistic traits affect daily life. | Difficulty with social cues, small talk, reading intentions, masking may be exhausting. | Resistance to change, intense interests, sensory sensitivity but generally manageable. | Some support needed, especially during stress or transitions. |
| **Level 2 – Requiring Substantial Support** | Marked difficulties socially and in flexible behaviour. | Limited reciprocal conversation, reduced non-verbal communication, noticeable social difficulties. | More significant rigidity, struggles coping with change, meltdowns/shutdowns more frequent. | Regular and consistent support needed in daily life. |
| **Level 3 – Requiring Very Substantial Support** | Severe impact on communication and self-management. | Minimal or no functional spoken communication, may rely on AAC/gestures. | Highly rigid behaviour, intense distress from change, high sensory vulnerability. | High level of support in most or all areas of life, often 1:1 care. |

---

### More Detail:

### **ASD Level 1 (formerly synonymous with “Asperger’s”)**
- Normal to high verbal ability.
- May appear “fine” externally due to masking/camouflage.
- Struggles tend to appear in:
  - communication nuances
  - social energy drain
  - executive functioning (organisation, planning)
  - sensory overload
- Often misunderstood as “quirky”, anxious, or introverted.

### **ASD Level 2**
- Communication breakdown more visible.
- May speak in short sentences, take things literally, or rely on scripts.
- Sensory issues and routine disruption can trigger shutdowns or meltdowns.
- Needs structured environments and support to manage daily tasks or employment.

### **ASD Level 3**
- Communication challenges are profound.
- May need assistance with feeding, hygiene, transitions, safety awareness.
- Behaviours such as stimming, self-soothing, or distress responses are more intense.
- Often requires lifelong support networks.

## A helpful way to visualise the difference:
Imagine three people facing the same social/environmental demands:

| Situation Example | Level 1 | Level 2 | Level 3 |
|------------------|---------|---------|---------|
| Unexpected schedule change | Stressful but manageable with coping strategies; may need recovery time. | Very distressing, may need guidance and support to adapt. | Overwhelming—could trigger meltdown/shutdown without significant support. |
| Conversation in a group | Possible but draining; may miss cues. | Difficult; may withdraw or struggle to participate. | Often not possible without assisted communication. |

### Important Notes
- **Levels describe support needs, not ability or intelligence.**
- Someone may **move between levels** depending on stress, environment, age, or burnout.
- Many adults diagnosed late in life (especially women and AFAB individuals) mask so well they appear Level 1 externally, yet internally experience Level 2-level struggle.

# **ASD, ADHD & Comorbidity — Educational Guide for Families**
*Written to help parents understand the real internal experience and daily impact*

---

## **Introduction**

Autism and ADHD are neurodevelopmental differences that impact how a person processes the world — socially, emotionally, and neurologically.  
From the outside an autistic or ADHD individual may seem capable, articulate, or “fine”, but inside the mental load can be overwhelming. The effort required to exist in a neurotypical world can be exhausting, and the struggles are often invisible.

This guide is written in an accessible, compassionate tone to help parents understand the **severity, complexity and emotional reality** of what their adult child may be experiencing — even if they do not always show it externally.

---

## **1. Autism Levels 1–3 Explained**

Autism is not “mild to severe,” it is a spectrum of **support needs**, not ability or intelligence.  
Someone can function well in some areas while struggling profoundly in others.

### **Level 1 — Requiring Support (previously Asperger’s)**  
• High masking ability — appears “fine” but internally overwhelmed  
• Social interaction requires conscious effort  
• Sensory overload common (noise, crowds, change)  
• Needs routine, predictability and recovery time  
• Can maintain independence but with significant internal cost  

### **Level 2 — Requiring Substantial Support**  
• Social communication difficulties are visible  
• Struggles to adapt to change without distress  
• Shutdowns/meltdowns require intervention and support  
• Sensory input is overwhelming and disruptive  
• Daily support often needed  

### **Level 3 — Requiring Very Substantial Support**  
• Limited communication — may rely on alternative methods  
• High distress from change or sensory overload  
• Requires support for daily living tasks  
• Needs a structured environment to function safely  

**Important:**  
Someone diagnosed as Level 1 may still experience intense internal suffering.  
High-functioning does **not** mean low struggle — it often means high masking.

---

## **2. Autism in Daily Life (What people don’t see)**

Autistic adults often spend every moment scanning, interpreting, analysing and holding themselves together. Things non-autistic people do automatically may require energy and conscious processing.

Examples:

• Difficulty interpreting tone, subtext or hidden meaning  
• Overthinking social interactions for hours or days afterwards  
• Feeling overwhelmed by noise, uncertainty or expectations  
• Needing recovery time after social events  
• Feeling “different but unable to explain why”  

Functioning does **not** equal coping.

A person may appear calm while internally drowning.

---

## **3. ADHD & AuDHD (Autism + ADHD together)**

ADHD affects motivation, impulse control, focus regulation and executive function.  
When ADHD and Autism coexist (AuDHD), the experience can feel like:

> “A racing brain trapped in a world that is too loud.”

Traits include:

• Can hyperfocus for hours but struggle with basic tasks  
• Knows what needs doing but can’t start (“task paralysis”)  
• Time blindness — minutes feel like seconds or hours  
• Emotional responses intense, fast, hard to regulate  
• Restlessness, thinking too fast to relax  

It is **not** laziness.  
It is the brain struggling to transition, plan, prioritise, and maintain rhythm.

---

## **4. Depression in ASD/ADHD**

Depression is **common** in neurodivergent people — not as weakness, but as the result of:

• Constant masking  
• Feeling misunderstood  
• Burnout from surviving a demanding world  
• Repeated rejection, shame, or social failure  
• Fatigue from “forcing normal”  

Signs may look different than typical depression:

• Withdrawing to feel safe  
• Not caring about previously loved interests  
• Emotional shutdown — “empty inside”  
• Motivation disappears even with desire to act  
• Feeling hopeless, stuck, numb, or overwhelmed  

It is not a choice — it is exhaustion of the nervous system.

---

## **5. Substance Use & Addiction Links**

Many autistic/ADHD adults turn to substances as **coping or self-medication**, not to rebel.

Reasons include:

• Alcohol/drugs reduce anxiety in social settings  
• Numbs emotional pain or overwhelm  
• ADHD brains seek dopamine (“normal baseline feels low”)  
• Helps switch off intrusive thoughts or tension  
• Creates temporary sense of belonging, quiet, or relief  

This is not about “bad behaviour” — it is about survival.

Understanding the “why” allows support instead of judgment.

---

## **6. Meltdowns, Shutdowns & Overload**

Meltdown ≠ being angry.  
Shutdown ≠ lazy or ignoring.

They are neurological events caused by overload.

**Meltdown — outward overload release**  
• crying, shouting, pacing, panic  
• desperate attempt to regulate sensory/emotional overload  

**Shutdown — inward collapse**  
• silence, withdrawal, inability to respond  
• body goes into “power saving mode” to survive  

Think of it like a fuse blowing under too much current.  
It's not a choice — it’s a system crash.

---

## **7. What Parents Need to Understand**

The most powerful help a family can give is:

• **Believe** their internal pain without minimising  
• **Listen** without trying to fix or respond with criticism  
• **Reduce pressure** instead of pushing harder  
• **Offer calm environments & routine**  
• **Accept** their needs, not judge them  
• **Be patient — not everyone can “just cope” like others**  

You do not need to understand everything,  
but **understanding they are struggling is everything.**

---

## **8. How You Can Support Your Child**

### **Do:**
✔ Ask what environments are comfortable  
✔ Give time, space and recovery periods  
✔ Use clear communication, not assumptions  
✔ Validate feelings — even if you don’t understand them  
✔ Encourage regulation strategies (quiet space, headphones, routine)  

### **Avoid:**
✘ “Just try harder”  
✘ “Everyone feels like that”  
✘ Forcing socialising  
✘ Comparing them to others  
✘ Rushing decisions or change  

Support is not fixing them.  
Support is **making the world less overwhelming for them to exist in.**

Because when someone finally feels understood,  
healing becomes possible.

## **9. Understanding Asperger’s in Detail (Now Classified as ASD Level 1)**

Although the term *Asperger’s Syndrome* is no longer used diagnostically (now grouped under **Autism Spectrum Disorder Level 1**), the characteristics historically associated with Asperger’s remain valid and important for understanding lived experience.

People with Asperger’s often have:

### **Strengths**
• High verbal ability and vocabulary  
• Strong logical/analytical thinking  
• Deep focus on interests (can lead to expertise)  
• Honesty, reliability, direct communication  
• Creativity, pattern recognition, problem solving  
• Loyal and caring relationships when trust is established  

These strengths often go unnoticed because the challenges are internal and subtle.

### **Core Traits**
• Difficulty reading social cues, body language, subtext  
• Conversations can feel effortful or scripted  
• Struggle with small talk; prefer meaningful topics  
• Interpretation tends to be literal rather than implied  
• Eye contact may be uncomfortable or draining  
• Sensory sensitivities (sound, light, texture changes)  
• Strong need for predictability, routine and control of environment  

### **Internal Emotional Experience**
Even if someone with Asperger’s appears “high functioning”, their internal world may feel:

- Overstimulating  
- Confusing in social contexts  
- Exhausting to navigate  
- Full of self-doubt about fitting in  
- Like they are “doing life on hard mode while everyone else has instructions”  

Effort is **constant** — observing, decoding, monitoring behaviour, controlling stims, trying to appear “normal”.

The world is not automatically intuitive.  
Everything requires **conscious cognitive processing**.

### **Why it is misunderstood**
Because someone with Asperger’s *can* communicate, people assume:

“you’re fine”  
“you’re coping”  
“you’re the same as everyone else”

## **1. Overview of Autism Spectrum Disorder**

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition affecting communication, sensory processing, behaviour and cognitive patterns. It exists across a spectrum of support needs rather than a linear scale from “mild to severe.” Presentation varies widely between individuals. Cognitive ability does not determine support need.

---

## **2. ASD Diagnostic Levels (DSM-5 Classification)**

### **Level 1 — Requiring Support**  
- Historically associated with **Asperger’s Syndrome**.  
- Speech and language typically age-appropriate.  
- Social communication differences present but may be masked.  
- Routines, predictability and familiar environments reduce strain.  
- Sensory sensitivities are present and require conscious coping efforts.

### **Level 2 — Requiring Substantial Support**  
- Social communication challenges are noticeable without support.  
- Difficulties adapting to change lead to shutdowns or meltdowns.  
- Sensory input significantly disrupts functioning.  
- Requires structured support across multiple environments.

### **Level 3 — Requiring Very Substantial Support**  
- Limited communication or reliance on AAC/alternative methods.  
- High distress from sensory input and transition demands.  
- Requires support for daily living, safety and routine management.  
- Continuous supervision or support often necessary.

---

## **3. Asperger’s (Historical Term Neurologically Aligned With ASD Level 1)**

Asperger’s Syndrome was removed as a standalone diagnosis in DSM-5 (2013) and folded into ASD Level 1. The profile remains distinct in research and clinical observation.

### **Typical Cognitive/Profile Characteristics**  
- Often average or high intellectual ability.  
- Language development usually intact without early delay.  
- Strong pattern recognition and focused interest areas.  
- Analytical, detail-driven processing style.

### **Social & Communication Profile**  
- Difficulty interpreting non-verbal cues, tone and social subtext.  
- Literal interpretation of language common.  
- Social interaction may appear rigid, scripted or formal.  
- Eye contact may be uncomfortable or consciously controlled.  

### **Sensory & Behavioural Profile**  
- Sensory sensitivities (sound, texture, light, change in environment).  
- Strong preference for order, routine and predictable structure.  
- Narrow but deep interests, often developed to expert levels.  

### **Internal Presentation**  
- High cognitive compensation creates outward competence.  
- Masking often used to fit expected behaviour standards.  
- Effortful social participation despite external normality.  
- Internal strain is frequently invisible externally.

---

## **4. Masking (Social Camouflage in Autism)**

Masking refers to conscious or unconscious suppression of natural autistic behaviours to blend into neurotypical expectations.

### **Examples of Masking Behaviours**  
- Forced eye contact despite discomfort.  
- Rehearsed or scripted social responses.  
- Mimicking tone, gestures or facial expressions of others.  
- Concealing stimming behaviours in public.  
- Copying social norms rather than intuitively recognising them.  
- Smiling or appearing calm during distress.

### **Purpose of Masking**  
- To avoid judgement, misunderstanding or negative social reaction.  
- To appear socially competent or “normal.”  
- To navigate environments designed for neurotypical processing.

### **Effects of Masking**  
- Increased cognitive load and sensory exhaustion.  
- Reduced authenticity and self-expression.  
- Significant emotional and physical energy expenditure.  
- Correlation with autistic burnout, depression and anxiety in research literature.

Masking often results in external functionality coexisting with internal overwhelm or fatigue.

---

## **5. ADHD (Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder)**

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition affecting attention regulation, executive function and impulse control. Frequently comorbid with autism.

### **Core Features**  
- Variable attention: hyperfocus or inattention depending on stimulation.  
- Executive function differences affecting task initiation and organisation.  
- Impulse-driven behaviour and rapid cognitive patterning.  
- Restlessness (external or internal).  
- Time perception irregularity (“time blindness”).  

---

## **6. Autism + ADHD Co-Occurrence (AuDHD)**

When autism and ADHD coexist, features of both conditions interact.

### **Combined Presentation Characteristics**  
- Strong focus on interests contrasted with task inertia.  
- Executive function load increased due to dual processing demands.  
- Sensory input combined with distractibility increases overwhelm.  
- Emotional response intensity elevated under stress.  
- Research indicates higher incidence of burnout and depressive disorders in AuDHD profiles.

---

## **7. Depression as a Comorbid Condition**

Depression is statistically common in autistic and ADHD individuals. Contributing variables in research include chronic compensation, social misalignment with environment expectations and emotional fatigue associated with regulatory effort.

### **Observed Indicators in Neurodivergent Depression**  
- Withdrawal or shutdown as baseline protective state.  
- Reduced motivation despite desire to function.  
- Loss of engagement with previously intense interests.  
- Emotional numbing or muted affect.  
- Persistent fatigue and cognitive overload.

Presentation may differ from neurotypical depression, often less outward emotional expression and more inward collapse.

---

## **8. Substance Use in Neurodivergent Populations**

Substance use rates are elevated in ADHD cohorts, often linked to dopamine regulation differences. In autism, use commonly appears as coping through sensory/emotional dampening rather than novelty seeking.

### **Common Motivational Drivers in Research Findings**  
- Self-medication of anxiety, insomnia or intrusive cognition.  
- Dopamine-seeking feedback loops in ADHD.  
- Temporary reduction in sensory or emotional overload.  
- Augmented social functioning through disinhibition.  
- Escapism from routine burnout states.

Patterns differ: ADHD often stimulant-seeking, autism more solitary or regulatory.

# **Updated Section – AuDHD: Conflict Between Dual States (Autism + ADHD Co-Occurrence)**

Autism and ADHD can coexist within the same individual. Research shows high comorbidity rates. When combined, the neurological patterns of each condition often **pull in opposite directions**, creating internal conflict.

### **Autism vs ADHD Conflicting Drivers**

| Area | Autism Pattern | ADHD Pattern | Resulting Conflict in AuDHD |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Routines & Structure** | Prefers routine, predictability, order | Seeks novelty, change, stimulation | Wants routine *and* gets bored of it |
| **Focus** | Deep focus on specific interests | Scattered focus/variable attention | Hyperfocus on interests but struggles to shift to tasks |

### **Conflict Summary**

Autism seeks predictability.  
ADHD seeks stimulation.

Simultaneous presence produces a **push–pull state**, for example:

- wanting to start a task, but unable to begin  
- craving routine while resenting monotony  
- wanting social connection but finding it draining  
- needing structure yet resisting structure when bored  
- craving stimulation but overwhelmed by sensory input  

Both systems operate at the same time, often cancelling each other out or creating overload. Productivity may fluctuate — periods of intense capability followed by shutdown/inertia.

• emotional strain from effort-based functioning  

The conflict is neurological — not behavioural or motivational.
