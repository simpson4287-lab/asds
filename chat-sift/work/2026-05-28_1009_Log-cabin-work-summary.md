Yes — **that is generally okay**, as long as you treat the membrane as a **weather/wind layer being clamped by the timber**, not as a structural layer.

Your stack sounds like:

**cement board / outer board area → batten / air gap → semi-breathable membrane → studs**

Screwing the studs into the battens **through the membrane** is acceptable because the screw penetrations are compressed tightly between timber faces. That makes them much less risky than random exposed holes in the membrane.

The important points are:

**Use enough compression**
The stud should pull firmly back to the batten so the membrane is sandwiched flat. Do not leave it loose or baggy between them.

**Avoid tearing**
Pre-drill the stud if needed so the screw does not twist and rip the membrane. A small pilot through the timber helps the screw bite cleanly into the batten.

**Do not rely on the membrane for strength**
The screw must be properly engaged into the batten behind. The membrane is just trapped between them.

**Tape or seal if you are worried**
For belt-and-braces, put a small patch of membrane tape or MS polymer/compatible sealant behind the stud line before fixing, but honestly, where timber is clamped tight to timber, I would not panic.

**Keep the cavity continuous**
The bigger issue is not the screw holes — it is whether the stud or DPM/membrane blocks the ventilation path. Make sure the air gap still has a route up/down behind the cement boards.

So yes: **screwing through the semi-breathable membrane into battens is fine**, provided the joint is tight and well-clamped. It is a normal sort of compromise in layered timber/weatherproofing work.

[M12] AI (2026-05-29 13:22):
Yes, I’d question it too. **Expansion tape is nice, but it is expensive for what it does**, and in your build its real value depends on what job you are asking it to do.

For your wall stack, expansion tape is mainly useful where you have an **awkward moving/uneven gap** and you want a compressible weather seal without relying on a perfect bead of sealant. It helps with:

- uneven gaps between timber/cement board/frame
- draught reduction
- keeping wind-driven rain/splash out
- allowing a bit of timber movement
- making a neater “soft” seal where hard materials meet

But it is **not magic waterproofing**, and it is not a substitute for a proper lap/drip/vented cavity. If the cavity behind the cement board is already ventilated and protected, the tape becomes more of a draught/splash detail than a critical structural layer.

### Good cheaper alternatives

**1. EPDM / DPC strip**
This is probably the best-value alternative.

Use a strip of **DPC, EPDM, pond liner, or rubber flashing tape** as a gasket between timber faces or as a skirt/lap. Clamp it with battens or studs.

Good for:
- sealing between timber and board zones
- creating a flexible flashing
- stopping splash entry
- protecting timber edges

Less good for:
- filling irregular gaps by itself unless compressed.

### 2. Compriband only in key areas, not everywhere

Use expansion tape only where it gives most value:

- around window/door modules
- vertical joints exposed to driving rain
- awkward uneven junctions
- top edges where wind can push water in

For long hidden runs behind cladding/cement board, I’d be tempted to save the money.

### 3. MS polymer sealant + mechanical cover strip

This is likely what I’d use a lot of in your situation.

A bead of **MS polymer / Sticks Like / CT1-type sealant**, then a timber/plastic/metal cover strip screwed over it, gives a very robust seal.

Good for:
- visible or semi-visible vertical joints
- sealing board-to-timber transitions
- stopping draughts

Important: do not seal both top and bottom of a ventilated cavity so tightly that trapped moisture has nowhere to go.

### 4. Foam draught tape / closed-cell glazing tape

Cheaper than proper expanding tape. Use it as a **compressed gasket**, not as the only weatherproofing.

Good for:
- timber-to-timber compression joints
- hidden interfaces
- reducing rattles and draughts

Less good for:
- exposed UV/weather unless covered
- big wet joints.

### 5. Low-expansion foam — limited use

I would use this cautiously. It is cheap and fills gaps, but it can trap moisture and make future dismantling horrible.

Acceptable for:
- internal voids
- backfilling non-ventilated dry gaps
- places where water cannot get trapped

Avoid using it where:
- the cavity needs to ventilate
- water might enter and sit against timber
- you need movement accommodation.

### My view for your build

For the wall/cement-board areas, I’d prioritise:

**vented cavity + mesh + clean membrane laps + DPC/EPDM strips + MS polymer at key joints**

I would only use expensive expansion tape at **critical perimeter joints** where you cannot easily form a better mechanical lap.

So, the cheaper practical detail would be:

**DPC/EPDM strip behind the joint → clamp with timber/batten → MS polymer bead where needed → leave bottom/top ventilation paths open.**

That gives most of the benefit without paying expansion-tape prices everywhere.
