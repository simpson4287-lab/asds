A standout—and often underappreciated—fact is this:

**Substance misuse does not act on a uniform brain; it selectively exploits differences across neuron types, meaning the same drug can reinforce addiction through entirely different cellular mechanisms in different people.**

In practical terms:

- **Neuron diversity is extreme.** Even within a single brain region (for example, the reward system), neurons differ by neurotransmitter, receptor profile, firing pattern, metabolic vulnerability, and plasticity rules.
- **Drugs target subsets, not “the brain.”** Alcohol, opioids, stimulants, and cannabinoids each act preferentially on particular neuron populations (e.g., inhibitory interneurons vs. excitatory projection neurons), reshaping circuits rather than globally depressing or stimulating activity.
- **Individual risk is partly cellular.** Variations in neuron composition and receptor expression mean that one person may experience strong reinforcement, another anxiety relief, and another dysphoria from the same substance—despite similar use.
- **Addiction reflects circuit re-weighting, not loss of control alone.** Repeated exposure shifts which neuron populations dominate decision-making, stress response, and habit formation, often strengthening rigid, low-flexibility pathways.

The key implication is that **substance misuse is not merely behavioral or chemical—it is a cell-type-specific learning process**, with neuron diversity explaining why trajectories into misuse, dependence, and recovery differ so markedly between individuals.

Key points, stated precisely:

- **Biological reality, not ideology.** Human brains vary at genetic, cellular, and circuit levels. Differences in attention, sensory processing, emotional regulation, learning style, and social cognition are expected outcomes of that variation.
- **Includes both strengths and vulnerabilities.** Neurodivergent profiles often involve uneven cognitive landscapes—areas of heightened ability alongside areas of difficulty—rather than global impairment.
- **Commonly associated profiles.** Autism, ADHD, dyslexia, dyspraxia, Tourette’s, and related conditions are typically discussed within a neurodiversity framework, though the concept itself is broader than diagnostic labels.
- **Context-dependent impact.** Whether a trait is disabling or advantageous depends heavily on environment, expectations, and support structures.
- **Not a denial of difficulty.** Neurodiversity does not claim that challenges are trivial or imaginary; it argues that difficulty arises from a mismatch between brain type and environment, not from inherent inferiority.

In short:  
**Neurodiversity recognises that there is no single “normal” brain—only a range of functional configurations, each with distinct trade-offs.**
