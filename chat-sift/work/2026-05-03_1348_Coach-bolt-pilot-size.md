[M1] ME (2026-05-03 13:48):
I have a coach bolt. The shaft is about 5mm. What size should I pilot. Going into wood 

[M2] AI (2026-05-03 13:48):
For a **coach bolt / coach screw going into wood with about a 5 mm shank**, pilot it roughly as follows:

**Softwood:** use a **3 mm pilot hole**  
**Hardwood:** use a **3.5 mm pilot hole**

If it is a **true coach bolt with a nut on the other side**, then the timber hole should normally be a **clearance hole**, around **5.5–6 mm**, because the bolt is not cutting into the wood.

If it is a **coach screw / lag screw** that bites into the timber, then use the smaller pilot above.

Rule of thumb: pilot hole should be about **60–70% of the screw’s outside thread diameter** in softwood, and a bit larger in hardwood. For a 5 mm screw, **3 mm is the safe starting point**.

For that one, assuming the **outside thread diameter is about 5 mm**:

- **Softwood:** **3 mm pilot**
- **Hardwood or very dry timber:** **3.5 mm pilot**
- **Clearance hole through the first piece, if clamping two pieces together:** **5–5.5 mm** through the top piece, then **3–3.5 mm** into the receiving timber.

I’d start with **3 mm** unless the timber is splitting or it feels brutally tight. Add a washer under the hex head if you want better pull-down and less crushing.

**Good use:** bedding, anti-rattle, extra grab, damp/exterior-ish timber, boards to battens, packers, trim, non-precision fixing.

**Not ideal as the only fixing:** structural timber joints, anything taking shear/load, anything overhead, or where you need a rigid wood-glue type bond. For your coach screw situation: **yes, use Sticks-All plus the coach screw**. Put a few beads/dabs between the timber faces, clamp/screw it up, and let the screw provide the mechanical restraint while the adhesive beds the joint.

For strongest wood-to-wood in dry, close-fitting timber, **PVA wood glue / PU wood adhesive** is usually better. For rough outdoor DIY fixing, **Sticks-All + screws** is a very sensible combination.

[M12] AI (2026-05-03 18:48):
Yes, it **does grab**, but not like a proper **high-grab construction adhesive**.

**Sticks-All / Sticks Like** is usually more of a **hybrid polymer sealant-adhesive**: very sticky, flexible, waterproof-ish, good on awkward materials, but it can still allow parts to **creep or slide** until it cures — especially if the pieces are vertical, heavy, or under spring pressure.

For **wood-to-wood**, I’d use:

**Best general option:**  
Use a **proper grab adhesive** for the initial hold, plus screws.

**Better mixed approach:**  
Do **not mix them together into one bead**. Instead use them in separate beads/dabs:

- **Grab adhesive**: a few vertical beads/dabs for instant tack.
- **Sticks-All**: separate beads/dabs where you want flexibility, gap filling, weather resistance, or sealing.
- Then screw/clamp the timber while it cures.

The screw is still the real restraint. The adhesive is mainly bedding, anti-rattle, and extra surface bond.

For your job, I’d do: **grab adhesive + coach screws**, and only add Sticks-All if the timber is damp-ish, uneven, exterior-facing, or you also want it to seal the joint.

[M14] AI (2026-05-03 18:52):
Yes, that’s a sensible approach for **light pieces on a gate**, provided they are not taking load or being used as a handle/stop.

Your bead layout is good:

- **Thick bead of grab adhesive in the middle** = initial hold and body.
- **Sticks-All around the perimeter** = sealing, weather resistance, and extra bond.
- Keep the perimeter bead slightly **inboard from the edge**, so it doesn’t squeeze out everywhere.

For **traditional solvent-based grab adhesive**, the usual method is often:

1. Apply adhesive in beads/dabs.
2. Press the piece firmly into place.
3. Pull it back off after a minute or two.
4. Let the adhesive “flash off” / become tacky for a few minutes.
5. Refit and press firmly.

That works because the adhesive skins/tacks up and gives better instant grab.

But check the tube: some modern **high-grab polymer adhesives** are intended to be pressed once and left alone. If yours specifically says “press, remove, wait, then refit,” do that.

For a gate, I’d still add **temporary support** while curing: masking tape, a clamp, wedge, or a couple of panel pins if acceptable. Gate movement, vibration, moisture, and sun can defeat adhesive-only joints before they cure. Clean/dry timber matters a lot. Scrape off loose paint or dirt, roughen glossy paint, then wipe dust away before bonding.
