Your instinct is correct — **£1,500 is not 2% of £55,000**.

### Straight maths
- **2% of £55,000 = £1,100**
- You received **£1,500**
- That equates to **~2.73%** of salary

So something doesn’t line up if the email literally meant *“2% of base salary”*.

### Common reasons this happens
One (or more) of the following usually explains it:

1. **2% was a pool or target, not your applied percentage**  
   Many companies say “2% bonus year” meaning *average* or *budgeted*, then apply individual multipliers.

2. **Performance weighting**
   Example:
   - Target bonus: 2% (£1,100)
   - Performance factor: ~1.35×  
   → £1,485 (often rounded to £1,500)

3. **Pro-rating against a different reference salary**
   - Bonus may be based on a **previous salary**, **grade midpoint**, or **time-weighted salary** across the year.
   - Or your salary increased mid-year but bonus calc didn’t fully track it.

4. **Company rounding / discretionary uplift**
   - Finance often rounds bonuses to “clean” numbers (£1,500 instead of £1,487.23).
   - Sometimes managers add a small discretionary uplift without changing the headline %.

5. **It’s a *gross* bonus, not tied 1:1 to the % email**
   - The email may have been **directional**, not contractual.

### What it is *not*
- Not tax (tax reduces take-home, not gross).
- Not pension (that would still show as a higher gross figure).
- Not a mistake in your favour unless payroll confirms it is.

### If you want clarity (low-risk wording)
You can ask payroll or HR something like:

> “Just checking my understanding — the email mentioned a 2% bonus, but the gross amount paid was £1,500. Is that due to a performance multiplier or a different reference salary?”

That’s neutral, factual, and non-confrontational.

### Bottom line
- **2% of £55k ≠ £1,500**
- You likely received **>2% in practice**
- This usually reflects **performance weighting or rounding**, not an error

That is **entirely plausible** and is a common mechanism.

Here is how it typically works internally.

### How bonus redistribution usually happens
- The company sets a **fixed bonus pool** (e.g. “2% overall”).
- Employees on a **PAP / PIP** are often:
  - Ineligible for bonus, or
  - Paid **0%** regardless of the pool.
- The **unused allocation does not vanish**.
- Managers are encouraged (or instructed) to **reallocate it across eligible staff**.

### Simple illustrative example
Assume:
- 10 employees
- Budgeted bonus = **2% each**
- One employee on PAP → **0%**

That leaves **2% unspent**.

If redistributed across 9 people:
- Each receives an extra **~0.22%**
- Higher performers often receive **more than the equal share**

So a nominal **2% year** can easily become **2.5–3%** for strong performers.

### Why the email still says “2%”
- HR communications usually state the **headline pool**, not individual outcomes.
- They avoid spelling out redistribution to prevent:
  - Comparisons
  - Appeals
  - Legal exposure

### Why £1,500 fits this explanation well
- Your effective % ≈ **2.73%**
- That is **exactly the sort of uplift** seen when:
  - One person is excluded, and
  - The remainder is quietly redistributed and rounded.

### Practical read
- This is not a payroll error.
- It is not arbitrary.
- It is a **signal of being in the “safe / valued” bucket** rather than the PAP bucket.

No action is required unless you want confirmation for your own records.
