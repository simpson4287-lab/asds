### 🧸 Typical Development
It’s very common — and **developmentally normal** — for children to have a strong attachment to a comfort object (like a blanket or stuffed toy), especially between **ages 1–4**. These “transitional objects” help them **self-soothe**, **feel safe**, and **manage separation anxiety**. Most children gradually grow out of it as emotional regulation and independence develop.

### 🧩 In ASD Context
An **obsession or fixation** with a blanket (or any object) might be seen as a **possible ASD-related behaviour** if:
- The attachment is **extreme or persistent well beyond early childhood** (e.g., continuing into later childhood or adolescence).  
- The object becomes **part of a rigid routine**, and distress occurs if it’s removed or changed.  
- The focus is on **specific sensory features** (e.g., the texture, smell, or colour) rather than emotional comfort.  
- It’s **one of several repetitive or restricted interests or behaviours** (e.g., insistence on sameness, repetitive play, intense focus on certain topics).  
- There are **social communication differences** or **sensory sensitivities** alongside it.

### 🧠 Why it happens
In ASD, such attachments may:
- Provide **predictability and control** in an unpredictable world.  
- Satisfy **sensory needs** (certain textures or smells can be regulating).  
- Act as a **coping mechanism** for anxiety or overstimulation.

### ⚖️ Key Point
Having a comfort blanket **alone** does *not* indicate autism — but if it’s part of a broader pattern of **restricted interests, sensory seeking/avoidance, or emotional rigidity**, it could be *one clue* among others.
