When screwing 47x50mm stud into the 19x35 battens. I’m thinking 60mm wood screws but 4 or 5 diameter?

[M2] AI (2026-03-31 18:54):
Use **5.0 mm**.

For a **47 × 50 stud into a 19 × 35 batten**, a **5 × 60 mm wood screw** is the safer default because:

- **4 mm** will often work, but is a bit light if the batten is doing any real holding or if timber is less than perfect.
- **5 mm** gives better pull-in and a stiffer fixing without being overly brutal.
- **60 mm length** is sensible here. If you drive through the **47 mm face of the stud** into the **19 mm batten**, you get roughly **13 mm embedment** after passing through the first timber. That is not huge, but acceptable for light framing/batten fixing if there are plenty of screws.

A few practical points:

- **Pre-drill the batten** if you are near edges, especially with **5 mm screws**, because **19 mm timber can split easily**.
- Use a **clearance hole in the stud** if you want the screw to really pull the stud tight to the batten.
- If this is doing more than just holding alignment, I would actually be tempted by **5 × 70 mm** rather than 60 mm, because 60 mm is only just getting into the batten enough.
- Put screws in a **staggered pattern**, not in a straight line right down the middle if possible.

So the clean recommendation is:

**5 × 60 mm for light/normal use**  
**5 × 70 mm if you want a more confident fixing**

[M3] ME (2026-03-31 18:56):
Should I not be bothered about it going past the 19mm and into the cement board? It’s the 50mm side I’m going through also 

[M4] AI (2026-03-31 18:56):
No — **you do need to be bothered about that**.

If you are going through the **50 mm thickness of the stud first**, then a **60 mm screw only leaves about 10 mm** beyond the stud before the point exits:

- 60 mm screw
- minus 50 mm stud
- leaves **~10 mm penetration**

If the batten is **19 mm thick**, that means the screw may **not fully stay within the batten**, depending on exact seating, countersink depth, thread start, and whether the stud is perfectly tight already. It could easily end up **kissing or entering the cement board**, which is not ideal.

For this arrangement, I would lean more toward:

- **5 × 60 mm only if you want to avoid risking the cement board and the fixing is fairly light**
- **4 × 60 mm if splitting is a concern and loads are low**
- **5 × 70 mm only if you are sure a few mm into the cement board is acceptable**

The real issue is this:

- You want enough bite into the **19 mm batten**
- but not so much that you start stressing or punching into the **cement board behind**

Since cement board is brittle, you do **not** want to rely on it or be driving hard into it.

My practical take:
- **Best cautious choice: 5 × 60 mm**
- **Pre-drill**, especially into the batten
- **Do not overtighten**
- Try to keep the batten pulling load modest
- If possible, check whether there is any gap between batten and cement board, because even a few mm air gap helps

If the cement board is directly behind the batten, then **60 mm is about the sensible upper end**.

[M5] ME (2026-03-31 19:07):
Can you on the membrane with a staple gun before lapping with tape 

[M6] AI (2026-03-31 19:07):
Yes — that’s a normal way to do it.

You can **pin the membrane with staples first**, then **tape the lap afterwards**. That is often the easiest way to hold it flat and in position while you work.

Best practice:

- **Staple into the timber behind**, not just loose board.
- Keep staples **within the area that will be covered by the overlap/tape** where possible.
- Use **enough staples to hold it flat**, but do not pepper it excessively.
- Then apply the **correct membrane tape** over the lap on a **dry, dust-free surface**.

A few important caveats:

- If this is a **breather membrane / house wrap**, ordinary stapling is usually fine because the system expects taped laps to provide the main weather/air control.
- Do **not rely on staples alone** for sealing.
- If the membrane is under real weather exposure before cladding goes on, too many staple holes are not ideal.
- On awkward areas, some people use **cap staples or clout-cap fixings** because they reduce tear risk.

So in your case: **yes, staple first, then lap and tape** is sensible.

For the neatest job, I’d aim to staple the first sheet, overlap the second sheet properly, then tape the seam so the staples end up hidden within the lapped zone.
