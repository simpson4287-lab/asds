Why is it nearly impossible to fire someone 

[M2] AI (2026-02-22 12:42):
Because in a lot of places (and especially in the UK/EU context), employment law is designed to stop *arbitrary* dismissal. That doesn’t make firing “impossible” so much as **procedural**: you can usually dismiss, but you have to be able to justify it and show you acted fairly.

Here’s what makes it feel so hard:

### 1) The law doesn’t ban dismissal — it bans *unfair* dismissal
After a qualifying period (e.g., **2 years in the UK**), an employer typically needs:
- a **fair reason** (conduct, capability/performance, redundancy, statutory illegality, or “some other substantial reason”), and
- a **fair process** (investigation, warnings where appropriate, chance to improve, right to respond/appeal).

If they skip either piece, they risk losing at tribunal.

### 2) Process is everything (and it’s slow by design)
Most “hard to fire” situations are really “hard to fire **quickly**.”
For performance/capability, employers are expected to:
- define expectations clearly,
- support improvement (training, adjustments),
- document evidence,
- run a PIP or capability route,
- give reasonable time.

That’s months, not days.

### 3) Evidence must be defensible, not just “manager vibes”
Managers often *know* someone’s not working out, but can’t prove it cleanly.
Common problems:
- no contemporaneous notes
- inconsistent standards across team
- weak objectives/metrics
- previous good appraisals contradict the claim
- decisions look subjective or personal

### 4) Discrimination risk makes employers cautious
If the employee could be linked to a protected characteristic (disability, pregnancy, age, race, etc.), the employer needs to show:
- decision wasn’t discriminatory, and
- **reasonable adjustments** were considered (big in disability cases).

Even when the employer is right, the *risk* makes HR insist on belt-and-braces process.

### 5) “Protected conversations” and settlement agreements distort the landscape
Employers often choose a settlement agreement (“without prejudice” / protected conversation route) because:
- it’s faster,
- it avoids the procedural minefield,
- it’s predictable cost.

So you see fewer “clean firings” and more negotiated exits.

### 6) Union presence, internal policy, and reputation amplify caution
Even where the law would allow dismissal, employers may add layers:
- internal policies stricter than law
- fear of grievance escalation
- morale impact
- reputational risk
- precedent (“if we fire A like this, we must treat B similarly”)

### 7) It’s not impossible — it’s just easiest for clear-cut cases
The easiest to dismiss *lawfully* are:
- **gross misconduct** with strong evidence (theft, violence, serious breach)
- **redundancy** with proper consultation/selection
- **end of probation** (still can’t be discriminatory, but fewer protections)
- **repeat conduct issues** with documented warnings

Performance without documentation is the hardest.
