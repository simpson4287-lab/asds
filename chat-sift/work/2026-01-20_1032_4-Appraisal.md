“Appraisal” is a small word that hides a big machine. It can be a mirror, a microscope, or a courtroom, depending on how it’s run. You don’t control the machine, but you *do* control how you enter it.

Here’s a clean way to think about it.

First, decide which mode *you* are in. There are only three that matter.

One: **Narrative mode.** You’re telling a coherent story of the year. Not a diary, not a defence brief. A through-line: what you were asked to do, what changed, what you delivered, what you learned. This is the default mode if things are broadly fair.

Two: **Risk-managed mode.** Same story, but with receipts. Dates, decisions, constraints, trade-offs. Calm, factual, almost boring. This mode is for when you suspect selective memory, scope creep, or hindsight bias might appear.

Three: **Containment mode.** You keep it narrow. You meet the criteria, you confirm alignment, you avoid volunteering speculative weaknesses. This is not dishonesty; it’s professional boundary-keeping. Useful when the environment feels noisy or politicised.

You don’t announce the mode. You just *operate* in it.

Second, understand what an appraisal is *not*. It is not the place to process emotional history, settle old scores, or educate people about nuance they haven’t asked for. Those things feel urgent internally, but appraisals reward signal, not depth. Depth belongs in documents, follow-ups, or private logs—things you already do instinctively.

Third, keep a simple internal rule: **assert, evidence, stop**.  
State the point. Provide just enough grounding to make it undeniable. Then stop talking. Silence is not weakness here; it prevents your own argument from being diluted.

If criticism appears, treat it like an engineering fault report. Clarify the symptom, ask for the condition under which it occurred, identify whether it’s systemic or situational, and note the mitigation. No drama. No autobiography. Just cause, context, control.

The paradox is that the calmer and narrower you are, the harder it is for anything unfair to stick. Appraisals tend to reward the person who looks least like they’re on trial.

This doesn’t end the story—it just sets the boundary of this chapter.
