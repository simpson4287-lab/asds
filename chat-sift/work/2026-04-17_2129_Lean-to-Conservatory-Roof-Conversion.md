It’s **moderately hard if it’s a proper warm-roof conversion**, and **quite risky if treated as just “stick insulation under the old roof”**.

For a lean-to conservatory, the difficulty is less about the roof shape and more about whether the existing conservatory structure can safely carry the new roof build-up.

## The big answer

A lean-to is actually one of the **simpler conservatory roof shapes** to convert because it is basically a single-pitch roof. No hips, valleys, lantern junctions, or complex geometry. But a proper warm roof still needs:

1. **Structural check**
2. **Correct roof build-up**
3. **Condensation control**
4. **Weatherproof edge details**
5. **Building Control approval**
6. **Good integration to the house wall**

In the UK, replacing a translucent conservatory roof with a solid insulated roof normally needs a **Building Regulations application**, because the change affects structure, thermal performance, and condensation risk. LABC specifically says a solid replacement roof requires Building Regulations approval. citeturn858586search0turn858586search2

## Difficulty by approach

### 1. Insulating under the existing polycarbonate/glass roof

**Difficulty: low to medium**  
**Risk: medium to high**

This is the tempting DIY route: leave the roof in place, add battens, insulation, foil, plasterboard/PVC cladding internally.

The problem is that it can trap cold surfaces above the insulation. Warm moist air from inside can reach the underside of the old roof or frame and condense. That can lead to hidden mould, staining, water drips, rotten battens, or failed plasterboard.

This route is only sensible if treated as a carefully detailed cold/warm hybrid roof with a proper vapour-control layer and ventilation strategy. In practice, it often becomes a bodge.

**Verdict:** easy to do badly; hard to do properly.

---

### 2. Replacing the roof with a lightweight solid warm-roof system

**Difficulty: medium**  
**Risk: low to medium if engineered**

This is the normal “solid conservatory roof” conversion. The old roof comes off, a lightweight insulated roof goes on, usually with aluminium/timber framing, insulation, breathable membrane, battens, lightweight tiles/slates, internal plasterboard, and trims.

This is easier to justify because some systems have established Building Control routes, structural calculations, and tested roof build-ups. LABC notes that a solid roof replacement can improve thermal performance, but it still needs approval. citeturn858586search0

**Verdict:** best practical route if you want a real year-round room.

---

### 3. Building a traditional timber warm roof from scratch

**Difficulty: medium to high**  
**Risk: medium unless designed properly**

For a lean-to, this is very achievable for a competent builder/carpenter, but you need to get the structural side right. The existing frames, dwarf walls, foundations, wall plate, and house-wall connection all matter.

A typical warm-ish lean-to build-up might be:

Outside to inside:

- lightweight tiles/slates or standing-seam type covering
- battens/counter-battens
- breathable membrane
- structural deck or rafters, depending on design
- PIR/mineral insulation between and/or above rafters
- vapour-control layer on warm side
- service void if needed
- plasterboard/internal lining

But the exact build-up depends on whether it is a **true warm roof**, **cold roof**, or **hybrid compact roof**. The vapour-control and ventilation details change accordingly.

**Verdict:** very doable, but not something to guess.

## The main issue: weight

The existing conservatory may only have been designed for a lightweight polycarbonate or glass roof. A solid roof adds:

- dead load from timber/aluminium framing
- insulation
- plasterboard
- tiles or sheets
- snow/wind loading considerations
- load transfer into frames, dwarf walls, base, and house wall

This is why Building Control cares. The Planning Portal also notes that structural openings between the house and conservatory require Building Regulations approval, and solid roof changes can trigger further compliance issues. citeturn858586search1turn858586search3

For your sort of practical DIY mindset: the roof itself is not conceptually hard. The hard bit is proving the thing underneath it is not being overloaded.

## Condensation is the second big issue

A conservatory warm-roof conversion fails when people focus only on insulation and forget moisture.

You need:

- a continuous vapour-control layer on the warm internal side
- no obvious air gaps from the room into cold roof voids
- properly sealed penetrations
- breathable membrane on the cold side where appropriate
- ventilation if the design requires a cold void
- correct abutment flashing where it meets the house wall

A solid roof that is warm in winter but quietly rotting inside is worse than a cold polycarbonate roof.

## Planning permission vs Building Regulations

Usually:

- **Planning permission:** often not needed if it remains within permitted development limits, but can be needed if the appearance/height/location changes significantly, or if listed/conservation restrictions apply. The Planning Portal says significant structural or appearance changes can require planning permission. citeturn858586search3  
- **Building Regulations:** normally needed for replacing a translucent conservatory roof with a solid insulated one. citeturn858586search0turn858586search2

So the common mistake is thinking: “It’s only a conservatory, so it’s exempt.” It may have been exempt as a glazed conservatory, but once you put a solid roof on it, that exemption can disappear or at least need reassessment.

## My honest view

If it’s a cheap old conservatory with lightweight frames, I would **not** just build a heavy timber/tiled warm roof onto it without a structural check.

The best route is usually one of these:

1. **Use a lightweight certified conservatory roof system** and get Building Control approval.
2. **Get a structural engineer/builder to design a simple lean-to timber roof**, especially if you want proper plastered-room quality.
3. **If DIYing**, keep it very lightweight and treat condensation/vapour control as the critical design problem, not an afterthought.

For a lean-to, I’d call it **physically manageable but technically unforgiving**. The carpentry is not the scary part. The load path, moisture control, and compliance are.
