An **EMP** is an **electromagnetic pulse**.

It is a **very short, very intense burst of electromagnetic energy** that can interfere with or damage electronics.

Three useful ways to think about it:

- **What it does:** it can induce voltages and currents in wires, tracks, cables, antennas, and circuits.
- **What it affects:** radios, computers, vehicles, power systems, sensors, control electronics, and communications gear.
- **How it happens:** it can come from natural events or man-made sources.

Common examples:
- **Lightning** produces localized electromagnetic surges.
- A **solar storm** can create geomagnetic effects that disturb large power grids.
- A **nuclear detonation at high altitude** can generate a very wide-area EMP.
- Certain **non-nuclear pulse generators** can create more localised effects.

In practice, people often mean one of two things:
- **Broad public/disaster sense:** a pulse that knocks out electronics over a wide area.
- **Engineering/EMC sense:** a fast transient electromagnetic event that couples into equipment and upsets or damages it.

In engineering, **EMP** can also mean **Engineering Management Plan**.

That is usually a document describing **how the engineering work will be organised, controlled, reviewed, and delivered** across a project.

- roles and responsibilities
- design process and lifecycle
- requirements management
- configuration and change control
- verification and validation
- reviews and approvals
- risk and issue management
- interfaces between teams
- schedule, reporting, and deliverables

- **electronics / EMC / defence / survivability** → usually **electromagnetic pulse**
- **project / systems / programme documentation** → often **Engineering Management Plan**

Yes, also include a detailed section in this : Here’s the cleanest UK/GB-oriented go-to-market strategy for selling a lab electronics system while keeping the compliance burden as low as reasonably possible.

The headline is: do not try to “avoid marking” by clever packaging alone. Instead, structure the offer so you are genuinely selling a professional low-voltage subsystem / evaluation platform rather than a turnkey finished instrument. A finished product placed on the GB market must comply with the legislation that applies to it, and components can still count as products in their own right depending on the rules.  

1) Best commercial model

Sell an OEM / evaluation platform, not a finished bench product.

That means your offer is framed as:
	•	B2B only
	•	professional use only
	•	for laboratory evaluation, development, integration, or research use
	•	low-voltage DC powered
	•	not a consumer product
	•	not supplied as a complete mains-powered instrument

This is the most defensible structure because it keeps the compliance boundary tighter and avoids presenting the product as a general end-user apparatus. It does not eliminate all obligations, but it is materially better than shipping a polished “ready-to-use lab system.”  

2) Product architecture to aim for

The lowest-friction hardware architecture is:

External COTS certified PSU → your low-voltage module/platform → customer integration / DUT setup

Why this helps:
	•	The Electrical Equipment (Safety) Regulations apply to electrical equipment designed for use between 50–1000 V AC or 75–1500 V DC. If your own unit only accepts low-voltage DC from an external adapter, your custom hardware may sit outside that particular mains-voltage regime.  
	•	Using a reputable external PSU also keeps the hazardous-voltage boundary out of your enclosure.  

What this doesn’t solve:
	•	Your system may still be subject to EMC if it is apparatus, or a sub-assembly intended for incorporation by an end user, and can generate or be affected by electromagnetic disturbance. Manufacturers must perform an EMC assessment where the regulations apply.  
	•	RoHS may still apply if what you are placing on the market is electrical and electronic equipment, though there is a specific exclusion for equipment designed solely for R&D and made available only on a B2B basis.  

3) What to sell

A good package would be:
	•	controller / interface PCB assemblies
	•	backplane or cage electronics
	•	interconnect harnesses
	•	low-voltage input cable
	•	firmware / software
	•	integration notes
	•	test scripts
	•	schematics or interface control docs as appropriate

A higher-risk package would be:
	•	mains cable included
	•	internal AC-DC supply
	•	IEC inlet on your box
	•	finished enclosure marketed as a complete bench unit
	•	simple “plug in and go” user experience aimed at non-specialists

The more your offer looks like a complete standalone product, the weaker the “module/evaluation platform” position becomes.  

4) What not to include in the box

To keep the boundary clean, avoid including:
	•	a built-in mains PSU
	•	user-accessible hazardous voltages
	•	mains wiring hardware
	•	general-purpose consumer-style instructions like “plug into wall and start measuring”
	•	accessories that make it look like a finished appliance

If you do include a power supply, the safest version is usually:
	•	external
	•	off-the-shelf
	•	from a reputable supplier
	•	clearly specified as the required power source for the platform

That reduces electrical-safety scope on your custom hardware, but it does not automatically exempt the rest of the system.  

5) Positioning language to use

Use wording like this in the datasheet, quote, invoice, and manual:

Product name
	•	“Detector Evaluation Platform”
	•	“OEM Sensor Interface Subsystem”
	•	“Laboratory Development Module”
	•	“Professional R&D Test Platform”

Intended-use statement
	•	“Supplied for professional use only.”
	•	“Intended for laboratory evaluation, development, and system integration.”
	•	“For business-to-business supply only.”
	•	“Requires integration into a customer-controlled test environment.”
	•	“Not intended as a consumer product or general-purpose mains-powered instrument.”

Power statement
	•	“Designed for operation from an external approved SELV DC supply only.”
	•	“External PSU provides the mains isolation and AC safety compliance function.”

Responsibility boundary
	•	“Final system integration, installation, enclosure design, cable routing, and end-use compliance remain the responsibility of the integrator/customer.”

This kind of wording helps define the integration boundary, though the real-world facts of the product still matter more than the words alone.  

6) Positioning language to avoid

Avoid phrases like:
	•	“complete lab system”
	•	“ready to use”
	•	“plug and play”
	•	“bench instrument”
	•	“for anyone needing…”
	•	“universal test box”

Those phrases make the product sound like a finished apparatus placed on the market for end use, which cuts against the lower-burden strategy.  

7) Documentation pack you should still maintain

Even with the lighter-touch structure, keep a basic technical file with:
	•	product description and intended use
	•	block diagram and compliance boundary
	•	PSU specification
	•	BOM and materials declarations
	•	risk assessment / hazard analysis
	•	EMC design notes and any test evidence
	•	installation restrictions
	•	warnings and environmental limitations
	•	revision control and serial traceability

That is worth doing because if the product is still caught by EMC, RoHS, or another regime, you want evidence that you took a controlled engineering approach. EMC guidance requires an electromagnetic compatibility assessment where applicable. RoHS compliance also expects manufacturers placing EEE on the market to evaluate production controls.  

8) Sensible minimum compliance posture

Even if your strategy is “subassembly / professional platform,” the practical minimum I would aim for is:
	•	external approved PSU only
	•	low-voltage DC only
	•	basic EMC due diligence, ideally including at least pre-compliance thinking/testing
	•	RoHS-aware BOM control
	•	clear intended-use restrictions
	•	B2B sales only
	•	quote / invoice / datasheet / manual all aligned

That is much safer than trying to claim “it’s only a kit” while still shipping a near-finished system.  

9) When a “kit of parts” is reasonable

A kit is reasonable when it is genuinely one of these:
	•	an evaluation kit for professional engineers
	•	a module set for integration
	•	an R&D-only platform supplied B2B
	•	a collection of subassemblies that still require competent integration into a controlled setup

A kit is not a strong strategy when it is effectively:
	•	your complete finished system
	•	just unassembled to dodge obligations
	•	sold with all parts needed for straightforward end-user assembly and normal use

In that second case, regulators are likely to care about the substance of what is being placed on the market, not the packaging trick.  

10) Marking angle: CE vs UKCA in GB

If you do end up with an in-scope finished product, GB currently continues to recognise CE marking indefinitely for the relevant product regulations covered by the government’s 2024 changes, so it is not necessarily a case of “UKCA everything or nothing.” Businesses can continue to use either CE or UKCA for relevant products on the GB market.  

11) My blunt recommendation for your case

For something like a specialist lab setup, I would use this model:

Offer:
“Professional B2B detector/sensor evaluation platform”

Supply:
boards, cables, software, docs, optional fixture hardware

Power:
external branded SELV PSU only

Exclude:
mains integration, finished instrument enclosure, general end-user positioning

Paperwork:
state clearly that the customer is integrating the platform into their own lab/test environment and is responsible for final system configuration and end-use compliance

That is the most credible low-friction route I can see from a product-structure perspective. It is not a magic shield, but it is commercially and regulatorily much cleaner than “sell the whole thing as a kit and hope.”  

This is practical guidance, not legal advice. For a real launch, a short review by a UK product-compliance specialist is worth it, especially if the platform has high-speed digital I/O, long external cables, unusual bias voltages, radio, laser content, or anything safety-critical.

# Engineering Management Plan (EMP)  
## With Integrated UK/GB Go-to-Market and Compliance Strategy

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Purpose of the EMP
This Engineering Management Plan defines how the project’s engineering activities shall be organised, controlled, reviewed, verified, released, and transitioned into customer use or production support. It establishes the engineering governance framework for delivering a technically sound, traceable, manufacturable, and supportable system.

### 1.2 Scope
This EMP applies to the full engineering lifecycle of the product, including system definition, electronics, FPGA/firmware/software, mechanicals, test infrastructure, documentation, productisation decisions, compliance boundary definition, verification, release, and post-design support.

### 1.3 Objectives
The objectives of this EMP are to ensure that:
- technical requirements are captured, allocated, and controlled
- the architecture is fit for intended use
- interfaces are defined and managed
- design maturity is reviewed through formal gates
- risk is actively identified and mitigated
- verification evidence is generated and retained
- released data is configuration controlled
- the placed-on-market product form is deliberate, consistent, and regulatorily defensible

### 1.4 Applicable Documents
This section should list the contract, SOW, customer requirements, system requirements specification, interface control documents, quality plans, V&V plan, configuration management plan, applicable standards, and product compliance guidance relevant to the programme.

### 1.5 Definitions and Acronyms
Define all programme terms such as EMP, PDR, CDR, TRR, PRR, ECO, NCR, VCRM, DFM, DFT, SELV, EMC, RoHS, WEEE, CE, UKCA, OEM, DUT, and B2B.

## 11. Product Market-Placement and Compliance Strategy  
## 11.1 Purpose
This section defines the intended placed-on-market form of the product and the engineering, documentation, and commercial controls required to keep that market position coherent. This is not merely a sales matter; it is a design-control matter. The physical architecture, supply contents, documentation, customer positioning, and quote/invoice language must all support the same product boundary.

## 11.2 Strategic Position
The preferred go-to-market model shall be:

**Professional B2B low-voltage detector/sensor evaluation platform or OEM subsystem**, supplied for laboratory evaluation, development, integration, or research use, rather than a general-purpose finished mains-powered bench instrument.

This approach does **not** remove all regulatory obligations. It does, however, create a cleaner and more defensible product boundary than a polished standalone instrument, particularly where the supplied hardware is DC-powered from an external approved supply and is intended for competent professional integration. EMC may still apply where the equipment is liable to generate or be affected by electromagnetic disturbance, and RoHS/WEEE scope must be considered separately. citeturn225840search2turn225840search3turn225840search7

## 11.3 Target Market Definition
The product shall be positioned as:
- B2B only
- professional use only
- laboratory evaluation / development / integration use
- not a consumer product
- not a domestic appliance
- not a general-purpose finished bench instrument
- not represented as safe for arbitrary end-user deployment without competent integration

## 11.4 Preferred Hardware Compliance Boundary
The preferred supply architecture shall be:

**External approved COTS SELV PSU → supplied low-voltage platform/subsystem → customer-controlled integration environment**

This approach is preferred because the GB Electrical Equipment (Safety) Regulations apply to electrical equipment designed for use between **50–1000 V AC** or **75–1500 V DC**. Where the custom hardware itself only accepts low-voltage DC below that range, the custom unit may sit outside that specific voltage-based regime, even though other regulatory regimes may still remain relevant. citeturn225840search1

The use of an external reputable power unit also keeps the hazardous-voltage boundary outside the custom enclosure and reduces the scope of internal mains safety design.

## 11.5 Regulatory Positioning Principles
The engineering team shall assume the following:

1. **Electrical safety scope is product-boundary dependent.**  
   Keeping hazardous mains outside the custom hardware materially simplifies the safety case, but does not itself remove EMC, RoHS, WEEE, or other obligations.

2. **EMC may still apply.**  
   GB EMC regulations apply broadly to electrical/electronic equipment liable to generate or be affected by electromagnetic disturbance. A platform or subassembly is not automatically irrelevant to EMC simply because it is not mains powered. citeturn225840search2turn225840search6

3. **RoHS exclusion must be used carefully.**  
   The specific exclusion applies to equipment specifically designed solely for research and development and made available only on a business-to-business basis. If the product starts to look like a more general commercial instrument, that exclusion position weakens. citeturn225840search15turn225840search3

4. **WEEE may also need consideration.**  
   UK WEEE guidance likewise identifies an exclusion for equipment designed only for R&D use and only available B2B, but the actual product facts matter. citeturn225840search7

5. **CE/UKCA route must be determined from actual scope, not preference.**  
   If the product is in scope of relevant GB product regulations, GB currently allows either CE or UKCA for the relevant regimes covered by the 2024 changes. citeturn225840search8turn225840search0

- firmware/software binaries
- integration notes
- ICDs and test scripts
- optional fixture items clearly identified as support hardware

# ENGINEERING MANAGEMENT PLAN

## 1. INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 Purpose of the Document
This Engineering Management Plan defines the management framework by which the engineering activities for the project shall be planned, controlled, reviewed, verified, released, and transitioned into supported use. The document establishes the governance arrangements, technical controls, review structure, configuration discipline, and evidence expectations necessary to ensure that the delivered system is technically sound, traceable, manufacturable, supportable, and aligned with its intended market position.

This EMP is intended to ensure that engineering is not conducted as a collection of disconnected design tasks, but as a controlled lifecycle in which requirements, interfaces, risks, verification evidence, and release decisions are systematically managed. The document also defines the approach by which product architecture, supplied form, and customer-facing documentation shall remain consistent with the intended commercial and compliance strategy.

### 1.2 Scope
This EMP applies to all engineering work undertaken within the programme, including system definition, electronic hardware design, FPGA design, firmware and software development, mechanical design, test development, interface definition, design verification, qualification activity, product release, and technical support to manufacture or customer integration.

The EMP also applies to the management of engineering deliverables, customer-facing technical documentation, product-boundary definition, and any engineering decisions that influence whether the supplied item is properly classified as an evaluation platform, OEM subsystem, laboratory development module, or finished end-use product.

Where portions of the system are developed by subcontractors, partners, or internal support functions, those activities shall either comply directly with this EMP or be shown to comply through equivalent approved arrangements.

### 1.3 Objectives

- ensure technical requirements are identified, controlled, and allocated appropriately

- ensure design outputs are reviewed, verified, and configuration controlled

## 11. PRODUCT MARKET-PLACEMENT AND COMPLIANCE STRATEGY

### 11.1 Purpose
This section defines how the project shall structure the supplied form of the product so that engineering architecture, shipped contents, documentation, and commercial positioning remain coherent. This section is included because compliance burden is affected not only by how the hardware is designed, but also by what is actually placed on the GB market, how it is powered, how it is described, and what role the customer is expected to perform.

The purpose of this section is not to “avoid compliance” by wording alone. Its purpose is to ensure that the project makes deliberate, defensible engineering and commercial decisions so that the supplied item is honestly and consistently presented.

### 11.2 Strategic Position
The baseline commercial model for the project shall be the supply of a **professional B2B low-voltage laboratory development platform, evaluation platform, or OEM subsystem**, rather than a general-purpose finished mains-powered bench instrument.

This model is preferred because it is cleaner, narrower, and more defensible than presenting the supplied system as a turnkey finished instrument. The strategy depends on the real substance of the supplied item and not merely on labels applied after the design is complete.

### 11.3 Target Market and User Class
The target route to market shall be:
- business-to-business supply only
- professional use only
- intended for laboratory evaluation, development, integration, or research use
- not intended as a consumer product
- not intended for unsupervised domestic use
- not represented as a universal standalone instrument for arbitrary end users

The intended user shall be assumed to be a competent professional integrator, laboratory engineer, or technical organisation capable of controlling the surrounding installation environment.

### 11.4 Preferred Hardware Supply Architecture
The preferred product architecture shall be:

**External approved COTS SELV PSU → project low-voltage platform/subsystem → customer-controlled DUT/integration environment**

This is the preferred architecture because the GB Electrical Equipment (Safety) Regulations are voltage-scoped to electrical equipment designed or adapted for use between **50 and 1000 V AC** and **75 and 1500 V DC**. Where the custom supplied hardware itself only accepts low-voltage DC below that range, the project’s custom hardware may sit outside that particular mains-voltage regime, even though other regulatory obligations may still remain relevant. citeturn265934search1turn265934search5

Keeping hazardous mains outside the custom enclosure also materially simplifies the electrical safety boundary and avoids unnecessary complexity in enclosure design, creepage/clearance management, mains wiring, field safety assumptions, and internal AC safety evidence.

### 11.5 Regulatory Positioning Principles
The project shall work to the following principles.

First, the project shall not assume that use of an external power supply eliminates all product obligations. Even where the custom hardware is low-voltage DC only, EMC may still apply if the equipment is liable to generate electromagnetic disturbance or have its operation affected by such disturbance. The GB EMC framework requires an EMC assessment where the regulations apply. citeturn265934search0turn265934search11

Second, the project shall treat RoHS and WEEE as separate scope questions. A relevant exclusion exists for equipment specifically designed solely for research and development and made available only on a B2B basis, but this position must be supported by the actual design, intended use, and market behaviour of the product. It becomes less credible where the item is marketed or supplied as a broad-purpose finished commercial instrument. citeturn265934search15

Third, the project shall recognise that if the supplied product is ultimately judged to be an in-scope finished product under relevant GB product regulations, current GB policy continues to recognise CE marking for relevant products covered by the 2024 changes, alongside UKCA. Accordingly, the marking route shall be determined by actual product scope and target markets, not by outdated assumptions that UKCA is always mandatory for GB. citeturn265934search2turn265934search19

### 11.6 Supplied Product Form
The standard supplied form shall be limited to those items that support the declared role of a professional platform or subsystem. These may include control/interface PCB assemblies, backplanes, harnesses, low-voltage input leads, firmware and software, scripts, ICDs, integration notes, and fixture items necessary for professional evaluation or integration.

The project shall avoid expanding the standard offer in a way that makes the supplied package appear to be a complete end-user instrument unless that change is consciously approved and the associated compliance implications are accepted.

### 11.7 Items Normally Excluded
The default supply shall exclude:
- an internal AC-DC power supply
- an IEC mains inlet on the custom equipment
- internal mains distribution hardware
- user-accessible hazardous voltages
- general-purpose consumer-style setup instructions
- “plug into wall and start measuring” style operating guidance
- accessories whose principal effect is to convert the product into a finished appliance or universal bench instrument

Where an external PSU is supplied, it should normally be a reputable off-the-shelf approved unit, clearly identified as the required source for the platform. This helps define the power boundary but shall not be treated as a substitute for proper scope assessment elsewhere.

### 11.8 Positioning Language Control
The wording used in datasheets, quotations, invoices, manuals, websites, release notes, and packing information shall be centrally controlled and shall remain aligned with the actual engineering form of the product.

Preferred language includes:
“Professional B2B detector evaluation platform”
“OEM sensor interface subsystem”
“Laboratory development module”
“Supplied for professional use only”
“Designed for operation from an external approved SELV DC supply only”
“Final system integration and end-use compliance remain the responsibility of the integrator/customer”

The following wording shall normally be avoided unless a formal decision has been made to release a finished product:
“complete lab system”
“ready to use”
“plug and play”
“bench instrument”
“universal test box”
“for anyone needing…”

The project shall recognise that language does not override reality. However, inconsistent or overly broad marketing language can materially undermine an otherwise careful product-boundary strategy.

### 11.9 Engineering Controls Required to Support the Market Position
To support the declared low-friction market position, engineering shall apply the following practical controls:
- the standard product shall be DC-input only unless formally changed
- the standard product shall assume an external approved SELV PSU
- mains conversion shall not be hidden inside optional variants without formal boundary review
- power input, limits, and connector requirements shall be explicitly documented
- interfaces and installation assumptions shall be controlled through ICDs and manuals
- EMC-conscious design practice shall be applied from the outset
- BOM control shall support materials awareness and exclusion review
- serial and revision traceability shall exist for shipped configurations
- technical documentation shall state environment, usage limits, and competency assumptions clearly

### 11.10 Technical File Expectations
Even where the product is intentionally structured as a professional B2B subsystem or evaluation platform, the project shall maintain a minimum technical file. This file shall include:
- product description and intended-use statement
- product boundary diagram
- approved power architecture and PSU specification
- BOM and materials declarations where applicable
- risk assessment and hazard analysis
- EMC design notes and any pre-compliance or formal evidence available
- installation restrictions and warnings
- revision history and serial traceability
- approved customer-facing wording pack

This file is necessary because a disciplined evidence base is valuable whether the project ultimately relies on exclusions, restricted intended use, or a more formal conformity route.

### 11.11 Sensible Minimum Compliance Posture
Unless otherwise approved, the minimum posture for this project shall be:
- low-voltage DC input only
- external approved SELV PSU only
- B2B sales only
- professional-use restriction
- aligned datasheet, quote, invoice, and manual wording
- EMC due diligence, ideally including pre-compliance testing or assessment
- BOM review with RoHS awareness
- retained technical file evidence

This posture is regarded as materially safer and more credible than attempting to market a near-finished instrument as a nominal “kit” without corresponding engineering or documentary boundaries.

### 11.12 Decision Gates for Product Form
A formal product-form decision shall be taken no later than PDR and confirmed at CDR. The project shall explicitly state whether the release is:
- internal prototype only
- customer evaluation platform
- OEM subsystem/module
- restricted laboratory development platform
- finished commercial instrument

A change in declared product form shall trigger a formal review of technical, documentary, and compliance implications.

### 11.13 Escalation Triggers
The following changes shall trigger immediate review under this section:
- addition of internal AC-DC conversion
- addition of mains connectors, mains cables, or mains wiring hardware
- significant enclosure redesign that turns the product into a standalone customer-facing instrument
- widening of intended use beyond professional or B2B laboratory integration
- sale through channels inconsistent with a professional-only route
- major changes to cable lengths, emissions-sensitive interfaces, or external I/O
- addition of radio, laser, high-energy, or safety-critical functions

### 11.14 Governance and Approval
No external release shall occur until engineering, programme, and commercial owners have jointly approved:
- the intended product class
- the supplied boundary
- the standard customer-facing wording
- the minimum technical file
- the compliance assumptions and open issues

This approval shall be recorded as part of release governance.

## 12. QUALITY, SAFETY, AND ENVIRONMENTAL MANAGEMENT

### 12.1 Quality Assurance
Engineering quality shall be built into the lifecycle through checklists, peer reviews, release reviews, documented standards, and controlled evidence. Quality shall not be treated solely as an inspection activity after design completion.

### 12.2 Safety Management
The project shall identify product hazards, installation risks, operator assumptions, and environmental limits. Safety controls shall be reflected in the design, instructions, and release conditions.

### 12.3 Reliability and Obsolescence
The project shall manage reliability and lifecycle risk through sound component selection, derating where appropriate, obsolescence awareness, and maintainability considerations.

### 12.4 Environmental and Materials Controls
The project shall define storage, handling, and operating environmental assumptions and shall maintain awareness of material-related restrictions where applicable.

## 13. MANUFACTURING, SUPPLY CHAIN, AND HANDOVER

### 13.1 Prototype Build Strategy
Prototype builds shall be planned to support learning, controlled issue capture, and iterative improvement. Build feedback shall be retained and fed back into design maturity assessment.

### 13.2 Production Readiness
Where repeat builds are expected, the project shall define what constitutes production readiness, including data completeness, test provision, assembly documentation, and approved source control.

### 13.3 Supplier Controls
Technical and material requirements shall be flowed to suppliers where needed. Supplier-provided items that materially affect product boundary or compliance assumptions shall be subject to review.

### 13.4 Handover
A formal handover shall occur when the project transitions into manufacture, customer support, service support, or sustaining engineering. Handover shall include all data, constraints, known issues, and support assumptions necessary for controlled continuation.

## 14. TOOLS, INFRASTRUCTURE, AND RESOURCES

### 14.1 Approved Tools
Engineering shall use approved tools for design capture, simulation, source control, requirements management, test control, and release management.

### 14.2 Tool Control
Tool versions, access rights, data backup, and repository discipline shall be managed so that design reproducibility is maintained.

### 14.3 Resource Plan
The project shall identify the engineering resource and specialist capability required by phase and shall track key single points of failure in skills or ownership.

## 15. SCHEDULE, REPORTING, AND GOVERNANCE

### 15.1 Engineering Schedule
The project shall maintain an engineering schedule showing major activities, review gates, build points, verification milestones, and release dates.

### 15.2 Reporting
Engineering status shall be reported at an agreed cadence. Reporting shall include progress against plan, technical maturity, open risks, open issues, verification status, and release readiness.

### 15.3 Governance Forums
The project shall maintain formal governance forums for design review, risk review, change control, and release approval.

### 15.4 Escalation Routes
Technical, compliance, documentation, or schedule concerns that threaten delivery or product-boundary integrity shall be escalated promptly through the defined governance route.

## 16. PROJECT CLOSEOUT AND SUPPORT TRANSITION

### 16.1 Completion Criteria
Engineering completion shall be based on evidence that requirements have been addressed, releases have been controlled, verification is complete to the agreed level, residual risks are understood, and handover data is complete.

### 16.2 Final Deliverables
The final project package shall include all released design data, verification evidence, approved procedures, manuals, configuration records, and project closeout records required for the next lifecycle phase.

### 16.3 Lessons Learned
The project shall capture key technical, process, and organisational lessons so that future programmes benefit from the work undertaken.

### 16.4 Support Transition
Where the product enters continuing use, manufacture, or support, the project shall define the receiving function, scope of support, known limitations, and ownership transfer arrangements.
