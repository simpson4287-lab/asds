[M2] AI (2025-12-24 22:07):
Short answer: **yes, you *can* cut back cabin eaves**, but you need to do it **selectively and deliberately** so you don’t create water ingress, rot, or structural problems.

Below is the safe, practical way to think about it.

---

## What eaves actually do (and what you risk)
Eaves serve three main functions:

1. **Keep rain off the walls** (biggest one)
2. **Protect exposed timber ends**
3. **Provide ventilation / airflow** (sometimes)

Cutting them back is usually fine *if you replace those protections elsewhere*.

---

## When it’s generally OK to cut eaves
✔ Timber garden cabins / log cabins  
✔ Felt or shingle roofs  
✔ Non-structural overhangs (most are)  

🚫 Less OK if:
- The eaves contain **structural rafter tails**
- There’s an **integrated gutter system**
- It’s a **fully habitable / insulated building** with vapour layers

---

## The safest ways to cut eaves back

### Option 1 – **Trim + add a metal drip edge (BEST PRACTICE)**
image_group{"query":["roof drip edge metal flashing","garden cabin drip edge","felt roof drip edge detail"]}

**Process**
1. Cut the eave back flush (or nearly flush) with the wall
2. Add a **metal drip edge flashing** under the felt/shingles
3. Re-dress or re-felt the roof edge

**Why this works**
- Water is forced to drip away from the wall
- Protects the cut timber
- Looks tidy and intentional

➡ This is what I’d recommend if clearance is tight.

---

### Option 2 – **Cut back + add a vertical fascia board**
image_group{"query":["garden cabin fascia board","log cabin roof fascia detail"]}

**Process**
- Cut eaves shorter
- Fix a treated fascia board to the edge
- Seal and paint

**Pros**
- Keeps timber ends protected
- Clean appearance

**Cons**
- Still relies on roof covering being well detailed

---

### Option 3 – **Local notch / taper instead of full cut**
image_group{"query":["notched roof eaves","roof eaves clearance detail"]}

If it only clashes in **one corner or edge**:
- Taper or notch just that section
- Maintain most of the overhang elsewhere

This is often enough to gain **30–50 mm** without major changes.

---

## Critical protection steps (don’t skip these)

### 1. Seal all cut timber
- End-grain wood preservative
- THEN exterior paint / stain
- End grain drinks water aggressively

### 2. Ensure water sheds outward
- Roof covering must overhang **at least 10–15 mm**
- Or use drip edge

### 3. Avoid flat water paths
No horizontal ledges where water can sit against the wall.

---

## How much can you safely remove?
Typical safe reductions:
- **Up to 50–75 mm**: very low risk with drip edge
- **Flush to wall**: OK *only* with flashing
- **Beyond flush**: not advised

---

## Quick rule of thumb
> **If you cut it → you must redirect water**

Do that properly and trimming eaves on a cabin is completely acceptable 👍

[M4] AI (2025-12-24 22:13):
Yes — **that’s a perfectly valid and commonly-used solution**, *as long as it’s detailed properly*. A **drip edge → gravel trench** setup works very well for cabins and outbuildings.

Here’s how to do it so it behaves like a miniature soakaway and **doesn’t splash back or rot the wall**.

---

## The correct build-up (top → bottom)

### 1️⃣ Roof edge with controlled drip
image_group{"query":["roof drip edge metal flashing","felt roof drip edge detail","shingle roof drip edge flashing"]}

- Fit a **metal drip edge** (aluminium or galvanised)
- Roof covering should overhang the drip edge by ~10–15 mm
- Water must *fall clear* of the wall face

This stops water tracking back along timber.

---

### 2️⃣ Vertical drop clear of timber
- Aim for **20–30 mm clear air gap** from wall face to drip line
- This prevents capillary splash soaking the cladding or logs

---

### 3️⃣ Gravel trench (mini soakaway)
image_group{"query":["gravel trench drainage detail","foundation gravel trench around shed","perimeter drainage gravel trench"]}

**Recommended trench spec**
- **Width:** 200–300 mm  
- **Depth:** 150–300 mm  
- **Fill:** 20–40 mm crushed stone (not pea gravel)  

**Best practice (optional but ideal)**
- Line trench with **geotextile membrane**
- Fold membrane over the top before final stone layer

➡ Prevents soil contamination and keeps drainage effective long-term.

---

## Splash control (important)
To avoid water bouncing back onto timber:

✔ Use **angular stone**, not rounded  
✔ Avoid flat slabs directly under drip  
✔ Keep first 150 mm of wall above gravel clear and sealed  

If you want belt-and-braces:
- Add a **slight outward slope** to the trench base (1–2°)

---

## Timber protection checklist
Before final assembly:

- End-grain preservative on any cut eaves
- Two coats exterior stain/paint
- Bottom wall log or cladding **never touching gravel**
- Minimum **50–75 mm clearance** from ground level if possible

---

## Is a gutter still better?
For a cabin:

- **Drip edge + gravel trench** → perfectly acceptable  
- **Gutter** → only needed if:
  - Large roof area
  - Heavy rainfall runoff in one direction
  - Close to a boundary or neighbour

Your solution is common in Nordic-style cabins for exactly this reason.

---

## Rule of thumb
> If water **falls freely**, **drains immediately**, and **never touches timber**, you’re good.
