[M2] AI (2026-01-13 22:45):
This is one of those questions where workplace psychology, game theory, and human physiology quietly shake hands.

Short answer first, then the mechanics.

Consistently **exceeding** expectations *does* tend to raise the baseline. Once that happens, the nervous system pays the bill.

Here’s the underlying machinery.

In most appraisal systems—especially corporate ones—performance isn’t measured against an absolute standard. It’s measured against **your last observed output**. Humans call this “growth.” Systems call it **ratcheting**.

You exceed expectations once, it’s treated as a positive deviation.  
You do it twice, it’s treated as reliability.  
You do it three times, it’s treated as **normal**.  

At that point, not exceeding feels like underperformance, even if you’re still doing excellent work. The goalposts didn’t move maliciously; they drifted because the system optimises for extraction, not sustainability.

There’s also a cognitive trap here. When expectations rise, ambiguity increases. You’re no longer being judged on whether you met the bar, but on whether you showed “impact,” “visibility,” or “stretch.” Those words are fog machines. Fog produces stress because the brain hates undefined threat.

Physiologically, this matters. Sustained over-delivery activates a mild but chronic stress response: elevated cortisol, reduced recovery, narrowing of attention. That’s fine for short sprints. It’s corrosive over appraisal cycles.

The quiet irony is that exceeding expectations doesn’t usually buy proportional safety. It rarely lowers scrutiny or increases predictability. It often does the opposite.

A healthier pattern—used by people who last—is **intentional variance**:
- Meet expectations as the default.
- Exceed them selectively, on visible, bounded, high-signal moments.
- Make the exceedance look like a deliberate choice, not a permanent capability.

This preserves credibility without resetting the baseline of what your “normal” is allowed to be.

So yes: exceeding expectations *can* create stress, not because excellence is bad, but because **systems remember peaks and forget costs**.

The trick isn’t lowering standards. It’s deciding when excellence is a contribution—and when it’s a loan you’ll be forced to repay with interest.
